## 1. Adoption: who in the UK owns crypto and what they do with it

### Takeaway
The latest FCA survey (Wave 6, fieldwork August to September 2025, published 16 December 2025) puts UK adult ownership at 8%, down from 12% in 2024, with about two thirds of holders holding £1,000 or less. The older "12% / 7 million" figure is still widely repeated and is out of date.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Safe to teach (stable features of the research)**
- The FCA commissions a recurring consumer survey run on the YouGov online panel. Wave 6 fieldwork ran "between 5th August to 2nd September 2025", with a nationally representative sample of 2,353 UK adults plus a boost, giving 1,361 current or past crypto owners. Verbatim. — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- The FCA itself warns the survey is online only, excludes the digitally excluded, and "could overestimate the number of cryptoasset users in the UK". Verbatim. — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- The FCA uses "cryptoasset" rather than "cryptocurrency" "to reflect their current primary use as a speculative investment". Verbatim. — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- Owners skew male, younger, and higher social grade in every recent wave. — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)

**(b) Needs an "as of" date (all figures below: fieldwork 5 Aug to 2 Sep 2025, published 16 Dec 2025)**
- Publication date 16 December 2025. — [FCA research note landing page](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/research-notes/cryptoassets-consumer-research-2025)
- Ownership: "the percentage of the UK adult population currently holding cryptoassets has declined from 12% in 2024 to 8% in 2025", still "double the proportion" of 2021. Trend from Figure 16: 4% (2020), 4% (2021), 10% (2022), 12% (2024), 8% (2025). — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- Awareness: 91% of UK adults have heard of crypto (men 95%, women 88%). — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- Who owns (Figure 17, share of each group owning): all 8%; men 11%, women 5%; age 18 to 34 15%, 35 to 54 9%, 55+ 2%; ABC1 10%, C2DE 6%; white 7%, ethnic minority backgrounds 19%. — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- Holding size (Figure 41, current holders who gave a value, n=854): £100 or below 27%; £101 to £250 14%; £251 to £500 8%; £501 to £1,000 15%; £1,001 to £5,000 21%; £5,001 to £10,000 11%; £10,001 or more 5%. In 2024 the £100-or-below share was 32% and in 2022 it was 39%. — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- What is held (owners): Bitcoin 57%, Ethereum 43%, Solana 21%, Dogecoin 20%, XRP 20%, Cardano 17%. — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- Why people bought (Figure 21, multiple answers): part of a wider investment portfolio 33%; "as a gamble that could make or lose money" 30% (up from 26%, peak 40% in 2022); expecting to make money quickly 24%; influenced by friends or family 21%; instead of shares or other investments 20%; fear of missing out 19%; retirement or long-term saving 16%; to pay for goods or services 15%; day trading 10%. — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- How people buy: a centralised exchange 73% (up 4 points); a payment firm offering crypto such as Revolut 15%. Among exchange users, Coinbase 61% and Binance 44% have ever been used. Top reasons for choosing an exchange: ease of use 50%, reputation 39%, safety and security 34%, cost 30%. — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- How people pay: own disposable income or cash 76%; long-term savings 25%; previous crypto gains 19%; credit card or existing credit facility 9% (down from 14% in 2024); any borrowing 12% (down 8 points). — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- What people actually do with it (Figure 43, ever done): converted to cash 45%; exchanged for other crypto 37%; bought goods or services 17% (21% in 2024); bought other financial products 11%; "utility purposes" 7%; governance voting 2%; never used them 34%. — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- Staking and lending: 22% of users have staked (down 5 points); 44% have not heard of staking. 7% lent or borrowed crypto in the last 12 months; 52% have neither done so nor heard of it. — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- Stablecoins: 58% of users have heard of them (12% of the crypto-aware public); 51% of users picked the correct definition; a quarter of buyers bought a stablecoin, half of those to trade other crypto on a venue; 14% of users hold Tether. — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- Research before buying: online forums 36%, friends, family or colleagues 36%, social media 29%; 13% did no research (up 3 points). — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- Outcomes: 62% of holders report a net gain since purchase and 12% a net loss (2022: 32% gain, 45% loss). 70% of users report no problems; the most common problem is difficulty withdrawing to a bank account (12%). — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- Understanding: only half of users say they have a good understanding of how crypto works (down 8 points). The top reason non-owners give for not buying is not understanding it (22%), then "too risky" (17%), then no protection (12%). — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- Advertising: 36% of crypto-aware adults recall a crypto advert; 48% of those saw it on social media; only 36% recalled a risk warning in it. — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)

**(c) Commonly misstated**
- "12% of UK adults (about 7 million) own crypto." That was Wave 5 (2024 fieldwork), which also reported a mean holding of £1,842. — [Lewis Silkin summary of FCA Wave 5](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2024/12/02/fca-finds-crypto-ownership-continues-to-rise-as-it-delivers-plans-to-regulate-cry-102jpzb) (secondary; used to locate the FCA figure). Accurate version: the latest FCA figure is 8% (Aug to Sep 2025). — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- "Crypto is mainly used to pay for things." Accurate version: only 17% of UK owners have ever bought goods or services with it; cashing out (45%) and swapping for other crypto (37%) are far more common, and a third have never used it at all. — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- "Most holders have serious money in crypto." Accurate version: 64% hold £1,000 or less and 27% hold £100 or less (sum of Figure 41 bands). — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)

### Inferences
- About half of holders (49%) hold £500 or less, so the median holding sits at roughly £500. This is my reading of the Figure 41 bands, not a number the FCA states.
- 8% of UK adults is roughly 4.4 to 4.5 million people. The Wave 6 report does not state a headcount; press coverage used "roughly 4.5 million". Teach the percentage, not the headcount.
- The fall from 12% to 8% alongside larger average holdings suggests small, casual holders left while committed holders stayed. The FCA foreword says the same in words ("More people are moving away from small holdings").
- A survey snapshot in August 2025 followed a strong market; the 62% "net gain" figure will move with prices and should never be presented as typical.

### Gaps
- The Wave 6 report text does not state a mean or median holding. The 2024 mean (£1,842) is known only through secondary summaries here. Do not quote a 2025 mean.
- No Wave 7 (2026 fieldwork) was found. If the FCA keeps its pattern, one may appear around December 2026; check before publishing.
- No reliable non-FCA UK dataset on day-to-day crypto usage (for example merchant payment volumes) was found in this session. The web search budget for the session ran out before this could be pursued.

## 2. Regulation as of October 2026

### Takeaway
Today crypto in the UK is regulated only in two respects (anti-money-laundering registration since January 2020 and financial promotions since 8 October 2023); the full FSMA authorisation regime is legislated (SI 2026/102), has final FCA rules (30 June 2026), opened for applications on 30 September 2026, and goes live on 25 October 2027. Several widely reported proposals were dropped in 2026: the credit-card restriction and the Bank of England's £20,000 stablecoin holding cap.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Safe to teach**
- The FCA's own consumer line: "crypto is largely unregulated in the UK" and buyers "should be prepared to lose all the money you have invested". Extracted; page updated 29 January 2026. — [FCA InvestSmart: crypto basics](https://www.fca.org.uk/investsmart/crypto-basics)
- Crypto firms carrying on in-scope business in the UK have had to register with the FCA under the Money Laundering Regulations since January 2020. Registration is for anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing supervision only. Extracted. — [FCA cryptoassets hub](https://www.fca.org.uk/cryptoassets)
- The crypto financial promotions regime started on 8 October 2023. There are four lawful routes to promote qualifying cryptoassets to UK consumers: (1) communicated by an FCA-authorised person; (2) made by an unauthorised person but approved by an FCA-authorised person; (3) communicated by a crypto business registered with the FCA under the Money Laundering Regulations; (4) within an exemption in the Financial Promotion Order. Extracted; page updated 6 February 2026. — [FCA: cryptoasset firms marketing to UK consumers](https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/cryptoassets/marketing-uk-consumers)
- Promoting outside those routes breaches section 21 FSMA, "a criminal offence punishable by up to 2 years imprisonment, an unlimited fine, or both". The regime applies to "all cryptoasset firms marketing to UK consumers, including firms based overseas". Extracted. — [FCA: cryptoasset firms marketing to UK consumers](https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/cryptoassets/marketing-uk-consumers)
- Prescribed risk warning for qualifying cryptoassets: "Don't invest unless you're prepared to lose all the money you invest. This is a high-risk investment and you should not expect to be protected if something goes wrong." Short form where space is limited: "Don't invest unless you're prepared to lose all the money you invest." Extracted from COBS 4.12A.11R; verify wording and the accompanying "Take 2 mins to learn more" link text on the live Handbook before quoting. — [FCA Handbook COBS 4.12A](https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/COBS/4/12A.html)
- Ban on incentives to invest: a firm "must not communicate or approve a financial promotion which relates to a restricted mass market investment and which offers to a retail client any monetary or non-monetary incentive" (COBS 4.12A.7R). The FCA tells consumers that compliant crypto marketing should not offer "free gifts to join or refer a friend bonuses". Extracted. — [FCA Handbook COBS 4.12A](https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/COBS/4/12A.html); [FCA InvestSmart: crypto basics](https://www.fca.org.uk/investsmart/crypto-basics)
- Cooling-off: a firm must allow "a period of at least 24 hours" to elapse before communicating a direct offer financial promotion to a retail client, who must then confirm they wish to continue (COBS 4.12A.18R). Extracted. The FCA describes this to firms as a "24-hour cooling off period". My understanding is that it applies the first time a consumer asks to see a direct offer from that firm; confirm the trigger on the live rule before teaching that detail. — [FCA Handbook COBS 4.12A](https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/COBS/4/12A.html); [FCA: cryptoasset firms marketing to UK consumers](https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/cryptoassets/marketing-uk-consumers)
- Personalised risk warning shown before a direct offer: "[Client name], this is a high-risk investment. How would you feel if you lost the money you're about to invest? Take 2 mins to learn more." (COBS 4.12A.20R). Client categorisation (COBS 4.12A.21R) and an appropriateness assessment (COBS 4.12A.28R) are also required before a direct offer is acted on. Extracted. — [FCA Handbook COBS 4.12A](https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/COBS/4/12A.html)
- The FCA confirms the appropriateness test already applies: "Authorised firms are currently required to apply appropriateness assessments where a retail client's transaction in qualifying cryptoassets is prompted by a direct offer financial promotion". Verbatim, para 10.22. — [FCA PS26/13](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/policy/ps26-13.pdf)
- Non-handbook guidance on crypto promotions (FG23/3) was published 2 November 2023 and requires promotions to be fair, clear and not misleading. — [FCA FG23/3](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/fg23-3-finalised-non-handbook-guidance-cryptoasset-financial-promotions)
- Retail crypto derivatives are banned: since 6 January 2021 firms cannot sell derivatives (contracts for difference, futures, options) that reference certain cryptoassets to retail clients (PS20/10, published 6 October 2020). The FCA's stated reasons included "no reliable basis for valuation" and "extreme volatility". Extracted. — [FCA PS20/10](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/policy-statements/ps20-10-prohibiting-sale-retail-clients-investment-products-reference-cryptoassets)

**(b) Needs an "as of" date**
- New regime legislation: The Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (Cryptoassets) Regulations 2026, SI 2026/102, made 4 February 2026; main provisions in force 25 October 2027. Extracted. — [legislation.gov.uk SI 2026/102](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2026/102/made)
- New regulated activities inserted into the Regulated Activities Order: issuing qualifying stablecoin (art 9M); safeguarding qualifying cryptoassets and relevant specified investment cryptoassets (9N); operating a qualifying cryptoasset trading platform (9S); dealing in qualifying cryptoassets as principal (9T) and as agent (9W); arranging deals in qualifying cryptoassets (9Y); qualifying cryptoasset staking (9Z6). Extracted; article numbers should be checked against the instrument. — [legislation.gov.uk SI 2026/102](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2026/102/made)
- The same instrument creates designated activities for public offers of qualifying cryptoassets and admissions to trading, and a market abuse regime (insider dealing and market manipulation prohibitions). Extracted. — [legislation.gov.uk SI 2026/102 contents](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2026/102/contents/made)
- FCA final rules were published 30 June 2026 in five policy statements: PS26/9 (admissions, disclosures, market abuse), PS26/10 (stablecoin issuance), PS26/11 (regulated activities), PS26/12 (prudential), PS26/13 (Handbook application). — [Regulation Tomorrow summary](https://www.regulationtomorrow.com/2026/06/fca-publishes-cryptoassets-regime-policy-statements-finalised-guidance-and-two-further-guidance-consultations/) (secondary, to locate); the 30 June 2026 date is confirmed on the [FCA new regime hub](https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/new-regime-cryptoasset-regulation); primary texts: [PS26/11](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/policy/ps26-11.pdf), [PS26/13](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/policy/ps26-13.pdf), [PS26/10](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/policy/ps26-10.pdf)
- Application window: "The application period is open from 30 September 2026 to 28 February 2027"; regime start 25 October 2027. Extracted; page updated 16 September 2026. — [FCA: what you need to do](https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/new-regime-cryptoasset-regulation/what-you-need-to-do)
- Confirmed on schedule: "The application period started on 30 September 2026 and will close 28 February 2027". Firms that apply in the window can keep operating until their application is decided even if that is after go-live (saving provision). Firms that miss the window can only run off existing contracts and cannot take new UK customers (transitional provision). MLR-registered firms get "no automatic conversion" and must be authorised under FSMA. Extracted; page updated 30 September 2026. — [FCA: how the gateway will operate](https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/new-regime-cryptoasset-regulation/how-gateway-will-operate)
- A draft amending instrument exists: the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (Cryptoassets) (Amendment) Regulations 2026. As drafted it would exclude simple transfers and exchanges of UK-issued ("relevant") qualifying stablecoins from the dealing and arranging activities, add a matching Financial Promotion Order exemption, add a proprietary-trading exclusion, and make issuing a qualifying stablecoin a controlled activity for promotions. Status in the copy read: DRAFT, no made or in-force date. Verbatim. — [HM Treasury draft amendment SI](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69e6334d44a079b27f997f89/Crypto_SI_Amendment.pdf)
- MLR registration is hard to obtain: from January 2020 to end 2025 the FCA received 386 applications (195, 63, 51, 28, 29, 20 by year); outcomes were 62 approved, 14 refused, 44 rejected, 257 withdrawn, 9 pending. FOI release dated 30 March 2026. Extracted. — [FCA FOI2026/00528](https://www.fca.org.uk/freedom-information/information-authorisation-crypto-firms-registrations-march-2026)
- FCA enforcement volume on promotions: "Since the introduction of the cryptoasset financial promotions regime in October 2023, we have issued over 2,300 alerts, taken down more than 1,000 scam websites, and removed dozens of harmful apps." Verbatim, foreword dated late 2025. — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- Crypto exchange-traded notes: the FCA announced on 1 August 2025 that retail consumers can buy crypto ETNs from 8 October 2025, provided they are traded on an FCA-approved UK recognised investment exchange. "Financial promotion rules will apply so consumers get the right information and aren't offered inappropriate incentives to invest." "There won't be coverage from the Financial Services Compensation Scheme (FSCS)." "The FCA's ban on retail access to cryptoasset derivatives will remain in place." Extracted. — [FCA press release, 1 Aug 2025](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-opens-retail-access-crypto-etns)
- Buying crypto with credit: the FCA raised a possible restriction in DP25/1 (May 2025). In January 2026 it dropped the idea: "we do not propose restricting firms from accepting credit card payments or lines of credit from EMIs", noting 57% of respondents opposed restrictions and that credit-card purchase fell from 14% to 9% of buyers. It still says "borrowing to purchase cryptoassets is unlikely to align with the risk profiles of most retail clients". Verbatim, CP26/4 paras 5.1 to 5.5. — [FCA CP26/4](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/consultation/cp26-4.pdf)
- Retail crypto lending and borrowing (rules apply from 25 October 2027): permitted for retail clients with conditions. Firms must give clear information first, obtain "express prior consent" each time a service is provided, run an appropriateness test, and must not use their own proprietary tokens in these services. For borrowing: mandatory over-collateralisation; "negative balance protection to make sure that the retail client cannot lose more than the collateral they have specifically dedicated"; loan levels modelled so "a margin call or liquidation is not expected within the first 6 months". Verbatim, chapter 6. — [FCA PS26/11](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/policy/ps26-11.pdf)
- The FCA stresses that crypto "lending" is not lending in the ordinary sense: "the provision of the service involves a disposal of the assets to the recipient, where the assets then become the property of the recipient". Verbatim, para 6.5. — [FCA PS26/11](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/policy/ps26-11.pdf)
- Stablecoins, FCA rules for UK issuers (apply from 25 October 2027): backing assets "held on a 1:1 basis", held on a statutory trust for holders; holders have "the right to redeem UK-issued qualifying stablecoins at par value", paid "by the end of the next business day (T + 1) following receipt of a full redemption request"; issuers "are not permitted to pass interest or income from the backing asset pool to tokenholders". Verbatim fragments from chapters 4, 7 and 10. — [FCA PS26/10](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/policy/ps26-10.pdf)
- UK-issued qualifying stablecoins will be excluded from the "restricted mass market investment" definition, so the high-risk promotion frictions will not apply to them. Verbatim, para 1.3. — [FCA PS26/13](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/policy/ps26-13.pdf)
- Stablecoins, Bank of England regime for systemic sterling stablecoins: policy statement and draft Code of Practice published 22 June 2026. The proposed holding limits (£20,000 per person, £10 million per business) were dropped and replaced by a temporary £40 billion issuance guardrail per stablecoin; backing moves to 70% short-term UK government debt and 30% unremunerated central bank deposits; no interest to holders. Comments on the draft Code closed 22 September 2026; the Bank aims to finalise by end 2026 with the regime operating in 2027. No stablecoin has yet been recognised as systemic by HM Treasury. — [Addleshaw Goddard briefing](https://www.addleshawgoddard.com/en/insights/insights-briefings/2026/financial-regulation/sterling-denominated-systemic-stablecoins-bank-englands-policy-statement-draft-code-practice/); [The Block, 22 June 2026](https://www.theblock.co/post/405545/bank-of-england-drops-proposed-individual-holding-caps-for-sterling-stablecoins-sets-40-billion-issuance-guardrail) (both secondary; medium confidence)

**(c) Commonly misstated**
- "This exchange is FCA regulated." Accurate version: most crypto firms serving UK customers are FCA *registered* for anti-money-laundering purposes only. The FCA warns "just because a firm is registered, doesn't mean you'll have access to the FSCS or the Financial Ombudsman if something goes wrong". Full authorisation for crypto activities does not exist until 25 October 2027. — [FCA: crypto investment scams](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/crypto-investment-scams); [FCA: how the gateway will operate](https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/new-regime-cryptoasset-regulation/how-gateway-will-operate)

Promotions and anti-money-laundering are regulated now; conduct, custody, trading platforms, staking and stablecoin issuance become regulated on 25 October 2027. — [FCA InvestSmart: crypto basics](https://www.fca.org.uk/investsmart/crypto-basics); [legislation.gov.uk SI 2026/102](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2026/102/made)
- "The UK has banned buying crypto with a credit card." Accurate version: this was floated in May 2025 and reported as a plan ([Finextra, May 2025](https://finextra.com/newsarticle/45927/fca-plans-ban-on-buying-crypto-on-credit)), but the FCA decided against it in January 2026 ([FCA CP26/4](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/consultation/cp26-4.pdf)). - "The Bank of England will cap stablecoin holdings at £20,000." Accurate version: that was a November 2025 proposal for *systemic sterling* stablecoins only and was dropped on 22 June 2026. — [The Block, 22 June 2026](https://www.theblock.co/post/405545/bank-of-england-drops-proposed-individual-holding-caps-for-sterling-stablecoins-sets-40-billion-issuance-guardrail)
- "UK retail investors cannot buy crypto ETNs" and "the derivatives ban was lifted". Accurate version: ETNs on a UK recognised investment exchange have been open to retail since 8 October 2025; the retail derivatives ban remains. — [FCA press release, 1 Aug 2025](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-opens-retail-access-crypto-etns)
- "Retail crypto lending is banned in the UK." Accurate version: under the final rules it is permitted with consent, appropriateness and collateral protections, from 25 October 2027. — [FCA PS26/11](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/policy/ps26-11.pdf)

## 3. The line between education and promotion

### Takeaway
The legal test is whether a communication, made in the course of business, is an invitation or inducement to engage in investment activity; purely factual or educational content is outside it, but only if it has no promotional element. The FCA applies an objective test, treats any commercial interest (including affiliate links) as "in the course of business", and is prosecuting individuals.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Safe to teach / safe to rely on as the framework**
- The restriction: "a person must not, in the course of business, communicate an invitation or inducement to engage in investment activity" unless the communicator is authorised, the content is approved by an appropriate authorised person, or an exemption applies. Verbatim, para 2.1. — [FCA FG24/1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg24-1.pdf)
- "A breach of s21 is a criminal offence which is punishable by up to 2 years imprisonment, the imposition of an unlimited fine, or both." It "applies even where a communication originates outside the UK if it is capable of having an effect in the UK". Verbatim, para 2.4. — [FCA FG24/1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg24-1.pdf)
- "Any form of communication (including through social media) is capable of being a financial promotion if it includes an invitation or inducement to engage in investment activity. This can include communications through 'private' or invitation only social media channels, like chatrooms such as Discord and Telegram." Verbatim, para 2.8. — [FCA FG24/1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg24-1.pdf)
- Business test: "We consider that the business test requires a commercial interest on the part of the communicator. It is intended to exclude genuine non-business communications such as friends talking in the pub." Verbatim, para 2.9. "This does not necessarily have to be a direct interest and, in our view, is intended to capture any level of commerciality." Verbatim, para 4.18. — [FCA FG24/1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg24-1.pdf)
- Payment is not required: influencers wrongly "assume there must be direct compensation for an influencer's post to be subject to the financial promotion restriction". Verbatim, para 4.16. — [FCA FG24/1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg24-1.pdf)
- Memes count: "The use of memes in promotions is particularly prevalent in the cryptoasset sector." Verbatim, para 2.37. — [FCA FG24/1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg24-1.pdf)
- Audience size is irrelevant: "whether a communication falls within the scope of s21 is not based on the size of an influencer's following". Verbatim, para 4.5. — [FCA FG24/1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg24-1.pdf)
- Affiliate links carry two risks. First, a referral-link post can be a promotion the firm is "causing" (para 3.19). Second, "an affiliate marketer earning commissions by introducing prospective customers to an investment firm may need to consider whether that activity involves them carrying on regulated arranging activity by way of business", which without authorisation is a criminal offence under section 19 FSMA. Verbatim, para 3.23. — [FCA FG24/1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg24-1.pdf)
- What makes something an inducement (FCA perimeter guidance): section 21 is aimed at communications with "a promotional element"; the Treasury intended that "only communications containing a degree of incitement would amount to 'inducements'" and that "communications of purely factual information would not", "provided the facts are presented in such a way that they do not also amount to an invitation or inducement". The FCA applies an objective test: the communication must "have the purpose or intent of leading a person to engage in investment activity" and "be promotional in nature". Extracted from PERG 8.4.2G to 8.4.4G. — [FCA Handbook PERG 8.4](https://handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/PERG/8/4.html)
- An invitation "directly invites a person to take a step which will result in his engaging in investment activity" (PERG 8.4.5G). Only communications that are "a significant step in persuading or inciting" are inducements (PERG 8.4.7G). Plain directory-style listings are "sources of information" (PERG 8.4.9G). "Links which are activated merely by clicking on a name or logo will not be inducements" (PERG 8.4.11G). Extracted. — [FCA Handbook PERG 8.4](https://handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/PERG/8/4.html)
- How the FCA suggests non-promoters stay outside the regime (written for overseas communicators but instructive): "changing the form and content of their communications ... so that they do not contain invitations or inducements to engage in investment activity". Verbatim, para 2.50. — [FCA FG24/1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg24-1.pdf)
- The ASA separately expects anyone paid or otherwise incentivised to "label their content as an advertisement upfront (including affiliate links)". Verbatim, para 1.9. — [FCA FG24/1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg24-1.pdf)

**(b) Needs an "as of" date**
- FG24/1 was published 26 March 2024 and remains the current FCA guidance on social media promotions as far as found. — [FCA FG24/1 landing page](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/finalised-guidance/fg24-1-finalised-guidance-financial-promotions-social-media)
- Enforcement, week of 20 April 2026 (press release 24 April 2026): 17 regulators took part; the FCA "secured a guilty plea from Geordie Shore's Aaron Chalmers for illegal promotions on social media", began criminal proceedings against 2 more individuals, sent 4 targeted warning letters, issued 34 warning alerts and updated 14, and made 120 takedown requests covering 1,267 illegal financial adverts that reached at least 2,338,372 UK accounts. The FCA's line: "Financial services ads targeting UK consumers should only be made by FCA authorised firms, or ads that have been approved by an FCA authorised firm." It also says many finfluencers "act legitimately". Extracted. — [FCA press release, 24 Apr 2026](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-spearheads-global-action-stop-illegal-finfluencers)

**(c) Commonly misstated**
- "Adding 'this is not financial advice' or 'for educational purposes only' makes content safe." Accurate version: the test is objective and looks at purpose and promotional nature, not labels. — [FCA Handbook PERG 8.4](https://handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/PERG/8/4.html)
- "Only paid adverts are caught." Accurate version: "any level of commerciality" can satisfy the business test, including anticipated future revenue. — [FCA FG24/1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg24-1.pdf)
- "Financial promotion rules are the same thing as financial advice rules." Accurate version: section 21 (promotions) and section 19 (carrying on regulated activities such as arranging or advising) are separate prohibitions; an affiliate can trip either. — [FCA FG24/1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg24-1.pdf)

### Inferences
- A UK limited company's website is almost certainly "in the course of business". The site's safety therefore rests entirely on content not being an invitation or inducement. **[LAWYER]**
- Features that push content toward "inducement" on the FCA's reasoning: naming a specific platform or token with a call to action ("sign up", "buy now", "get started"), price predictions, comparative "best exchange" rankings, referral or affiliate links, sign-up bonuses, and urgency. Features consistent with "inform or educate": explaining mechanics and risks, neutral lists without calls to action, linking to regulators.
- A plain name-or-logo link is treated more leniently than a link wrapped in persuasive text; the surrounding words matter more than the link.
- Repeating the FCA's prescribed risk warning on an educational page is not required and could itself make a page look like a promotion that is trying to comply. Explaining what the warning is and why it exists is the educational version. **[LAWYER]**
- Because EndorseCrypto is neither authorised nor MLR-registered, it has no lawful route to make a crypto promotion itself; only approval by an authorised firm with the relevant approver permission would allow one.
- The brand name contains the word "Endorse". An objective observer could read that as signalling recommendation. **[LAWYER]** worth raising.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve FCA guidance aimed specifically at educational publishers or course sellers; the conclusions above are drawn from the general perimeter guidance and the social media guidance.
- The Financial Promotion Order journalism exemption (and whether a site like this could rely on it) was not researched. **[LAWYER]**
- The exact product Aaron Chalmers promoted, and sentencing, were not in the press release extract.
- PERG wording was extracted, not read verbatim.

## 4. Consumer protection: FSCS and the Financial Ombudsman

### Takeaway
Crypto holdings are not covered by the Financial Services Compensation Scheme now and will not be under the new regime; the Financial Ombudsman Service will be able to hear complaints about authorised crypto firms only for acts on or after 25 October 2027, and not for losses caused by price falls.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Safe to teach**
- The FSCS is "the UK's statutory compensation scheme. If a financial firm has gone out of business and can't pay a customer's claim, FSCS can step in". The Financial Ombudsman Service is "an impartial body set up by Parliament to resolve certain complaints between consumers and businesses that provide financial services". Verbatim, para 3.1. — [FCA CP26/4](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/consultation/cp26-4.pdf)
- For crypto, the FCA says "it is highly unlikely you will be covered by the Financial Services Compensation Scheme". Extracted. — [FCA InvestSmart: crypto basics](https://www.fca.org.uk/investsmart/crypto-basics)
- Registration does not bring protection: "just because a firm is registered, doesn't mean you'll have access to the FSCS or the Financial Ombudsman if something goes wrong". Extracted; page updated 16 February 2026. — [FCA: crypto investment scams](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/crypto-investment-scams)
- Dealing with an unauthorised firm means no Ombudsman and no FSCS cover. Extracted. — [FCA Warning List page](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/warning-list-unauthorised-firms)
- Crypto ETNs bought by retail investors have no FSCS cover. — [FCA press release, 1 Aug 2025](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-opens-retail-access-crypto-etns)
- Even where the Ombudsman has jurisdiction, "It is unlikely that redress will be owed in cases where a loss suffered by a consumer has been caused by poor market performance." Verbatim, chapter 12. — [FCA PS26/13](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/policy/ps26-13.pdf)

**(b) Needs an "as of" date**
- New regime, FSCS: "We do not plan to extend FSCS cover to new regulated cryptoasset activities." Standardised risk-summary wording will tell consumers crypto activities are not FSCS-covered. Verbatim, chapter 12 (30 June 2026). — [FCA PS26/13](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/policy/ps26-13.pdf)
- New regime, Ombudsman: the compulsory jurisdiction is extended to the new regulated crypto activities. "The Financial Ombudsman will only be able to consider complaints against qualifying cryptoasset firms arising from acts or omissions in carrying out regulated activities on or after the day the new regime goes live". Verbatim, para 3.24. — [FCA CP26/4](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/consultation/cp26-4.pdf); confirmed as final in [FCA PS26/13](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/policy/ps26-13.pdf)
- The Ombudsman "will not extend its voluntary jurisdiction" to the new crypto activities, and complaints from non-UK customers of overseas-incorporated trading platforms are carved out. Verbatim. — [FCA PS26/13](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/policy/ps26-13.pdf)
- FSCS investment limit, for context only (it does not apply to crypto): "up to £85,000 per eligible person, per firm". Extracted. — [FSCS: investments](https://www.fscs.org.uk/what-we-cover/investments/)
- Bank-transfer scam reimbursement (not crypto-specific): since 7 October 2024 victims of authorised push payment fraud paid by Faster Payments or CHAPS "from one UK bank account to another" can claim up to £85,000, within 13 months, normally reimbursed within 5 business days, with an optional £100 excess; not if "complicit in the fraud or grossly negligent". Extracted. — [Payment Systems Regulator: APP reimbursement](https://www.psr.org.uk/information-for-consumers/app-fraud-reimbursement-protections/)
- Public misunderstanding (Aug to Sep 2025): 13% of crypto users believe they would have financial protection (20% of users aged 18 to 34); 28% think they could bring a complaint; of those, 18% of users and 32% of non-users name the Financial Ombudsman; among non-buyers, 15% would expect FSCS protection. Verbatim. — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)

**(c) Commonly misstated**
- "Your crypto is protected up to £85,000." Accurate version: no FSCS cover for crypto now, and none planned under the 2027 regime. — [FCA PS26/13](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/policy/ps26-13.pdf)
- "Once crypto is regulated in 2027 you'll be fully protected." Accurate version: you gain a complaints route (firm, then Ombudsman) for an authorised firm's conduct from 25 October 2027; you do not gain compensation if the firm fails, or redress for price falls. — [FCA PS26/13](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/policy/ps26-13.pdf)
- "My bank will refund me if I'm scammed in crypto." Accurate version: the mandatory scheme covers UK bank-to-bank transfers; it is not a crypto protection. See Gaps for the unverified detail on transfers to your own exchange account. — [Payment Systems Regulator: APP reimbursement](https://www.psr.org.uk/information-for-consumers/app-fraud-reimbursement-protections/)

### Inferences
- A clean beginner framing: three different things are often blurred, namely *registered* (anti-money-laundering checks), *authorised* (from October 2027, conduct rules and an Ombudsman route), and *compensated* (FSCS, which crypto does not get).
- The FCA deliberately chose not to extend FSCS so as not to "send inappropriate signals about the risk profile of these assets"; that reasoning is itself teachable.

### Gaps
- The FSCS's own crypto statement page was not retrieved (guessed URL 404). The FSCS position is sourced via the FCA.
- Whether the bank-transfer reimbursement rules exclude payments to an account you control (for example your own exchange account before crypto is sent on to a scammer) was not verified in this session. This is widely stated and probably correct, but do not teach it without the PSR source.
- The Financial Ombudsman's own current page on crypto complaints (for example complaints against banks over scam payments) was not retrieved.
- Section 75 Consumer Credit Act and chargeback in relation to crypto purchases were not researched.

## 5. Tax: HMRC's treatment of crypto for individuals

### Takeaway
There is no special crypto tax: disposals (including crypto-to-crypto swaps and spending) fall under Capital Gains Tax with share-style pooling, and crypto received from work, mining, staking or lending is income. For 2026/27 the CGT allowance is £3,000 and rates are 18% and 24%. From 1 January 2026 platforms collect user and transaction data for HMRC under the Cryptoasset Reporting Framework, with first reports due by 31 May 2027.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Safe to teach**
- You dispose of cryptoassets, and may owe Capital Gains Tax, when "selling them", "exchanging them for a different type of cryptoasset", "using them to pay for goods or services", or "giving them to another person" (gifts to a spouse, civil partner or charity excepted). Extracted; page updated 29 May 2025. — [gov.uk: tax when you sell cryptoassets](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/check-if-you-need-to-pay-tax-when-you-sell-cryptoassets)
- Pooling: you must "group each type of token you own into 'pools' and work out the pooled cost for each type". Tokens bought on the same day as a sale, or within 30 days after a sale, of the same type are matched first and not pooled. Extracted. — [gov.uk: tax when you sell cryptoassets](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/check-if-you-need-to-pay-tax-when-you-sell-cryptoassets)
- Matching order: same-day rule first (TCGA 1992 s105), then the 30-day rule (s106A), then the section 104 pool. Non-fungible tokens "are separately identifiable and so are not pooled". Extracted; manual page updated 28 November 2025. — [HMRC Cryptoassets Manual CRYPTO22200](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/cryptoassets-manual/crypto22200)
- Allowable costs include "transaction fees" and "a proportion of the pooled costs"; mining costs such as equipment and electricity are not allowable for CGT. Extracted. — [gov.uk: tax when you sell cryptoassets](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/check-if-you-need-to-pay-tax-when-you-sell-cryptoassets)
- Records to keep per pool: type of token, disposal date, number disposed of, number left, value in pounds sterling, bank statements, pooled costs before and after. Extracted. — [gov.uk: tax when you sell cryptoassets](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/check-if-you-need-to-pay-tax-when-you-sell-cryptoassets)
- Income: "any cryptoasset exchange tokens ... you receive from employment, or from activities such as mining, staking or lending count as income". If you later sell tokens you paid Income Tax on, you "calculate Capital Gains Tax as normal on any increase in the value of the tokens since you received them". Extracted; page updated 28 April 2025. — [gov.uk: tax when you receive cryptoassets](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/check-if-you-need-to-pay-tax-when-you-receive-cryptoassets)
- Staking: whether it is a trade depends on "degree of activity, organisation, risk, commerciality"; if not a trade, "The pound sterling value (at the time of receipt) of any tokens awarded will be taxable as income (miscellaneous income)". Extracted; updated 28 November 2025. — [HMRC CRYPTO21200](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/cryptoassets-manual/crypto21200)
- Airdrops: Income Tax "will not always apply"; it may not apply where tokens are received "without doing anything in return" and not as part of a trade. Airdrops "provided in return for, or in expectation of, a service are subject to Income Tax". Either way, "The disposal of a token received through an airdrop may result in a chargeable gain for Capital Gains Tax, even if it's not chargeable to Income Tax when it's received." Extracted; updated 28 November 2025. — [HMRC CRYPTO21250](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/cryptoassets-manual/crypto21250)
- Reporting routes: a Self Assessment return, or the Capital Gains Tax real time service; earlier undeclared years go through HMRC's Cryptoasset Disclosure Service. Extracted. — [gov.uk: tax when you sell cryptoassets](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/check-if-you-need-to-pay-tax-when-you-sell-cryptoassets)
- HMRC on the reporting framework: "This isn't a new tax – if you make a profit when you sell, swap or transfer your crypto, tax may already be due." (Jonathan Athow, HMRC, 7 July 2025). Extracted. — [HMRC press release, 7 Jul 2025](https://hm-revenue-customs-hmrc.mynewsdesk.com/pressreleases/crypto-bros-being-forced-to-pay-fair-share-of-tax-3395840)

**(b) Needs an "as of" date**
- 2026/27 CGT annual exempt amount: "For the 2026 to 2027 tax year the allowance is £3,000". Extracted. — [gov.uk: Capital Gains Tax rates and allowances](https://www.gov.uk/capital-gains-tax/rates)
- 2026/27 CGT rates on assets such as crypto: 18% on gains falling within the basic Income Tax band and 24% above it; higher and additional rate taxpayers pay 24%. Extracted. — [gov.uk: Capital Gains Tax rates](https://www.gov.uk/capital-gains-tax/rates)
- Trading and miscellaneous income allowance: £1,000 per tax year; crypto income counts toward it. Extracted. — [gov.uk: tax when you receive cryptoassets](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/check-if-you-need-to-pay-tax-when-you-receive-cryptoassets)
- Cryptoasset Reporting Framework: UK reporting cryptoasset service providers must collect details of all users from 1 January 2026 and report between 1 January 2027 and 31 May 2027 for calendar year 2026. They report users "tax resident in the UK or another country that is signed up to CARF rules", with user details and "a summary of their transactions". Providers face "a penalty of up to £300 per user" for failures. Extracted; page updated 3 June 2026. — [gov.uk: reporting cryptoasset user and transaction data](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/reporting-cryptoasset-user-and-transaction-data)
- What users must give their provider from January 2026: name, address, date of birth, tax residence, National Insurance number or tax reference. Users who do not comply risk a £300 penalty. HMRC expects up to £315 million by April 2030. Extracted. — [HMRC press release, 7 Jul 2025](https://hm-revenue-customs-hmrc.mynewsdesk.com/pressreleases/crypto-bros-being-forced-to-pay-fair-share-of-tax-3395840)
- Self Assessment form: the SA108 Capital Gains Tax summary for 2025–26 (form dated HMRC 12/25) has a dedicated "Cryptoassets" section, boxes 13.1 to 13.8: number of disposals; disposal proceeds; allowable costs (including purchase price); gains in the year before losses; losses in the year; claim or election code; total gains or losses already reported on Real Time Transaction returns; tax on those gains already paid. Verbatim (section heading order inferred from the PDF layout). — [HMRC form SA108 2026](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69bd8990cfa346b9d47049e4/SA108-2026.pdf)
- The dedicated crypto section first appeared on the 2024/25 return; the 2025/26 online return is due 31 January 2027. — [Recap: Self Assessment for crypto](https://recap.io/blog/the-2025-self-assessment-tax-return-form) (secondary, commercial; medium confidence)
- DeFi lending and staking tax reform is still a proposal: HMRC published a summary of responses on 26 November 2025 that "outlines a potential approach the government is considering". Not legislated. Extracted. — [gov.uk: DeFi taxation consultation](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/the-taxation-of-decentralised-finance-involving-the-lending-and-staking-of-cryptoassets)

**(c) Commonly misstated**
- "You only pay tax when you cash out to pounds." Accurate version: swapping one cryptoasset for another and spending crypto are disposals. — [gov.uk: tax when you sell cryptoassets](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/check-if-you-need-to-pay-tax-when-you-sell-cryptoassets)
- "The tax-free allowance is £12,300 (or £6,000)." Accurate version: £3,000 for 2026/27. — [gov.uk: Capital Gains Tax](https://www.gov.uk/capital-gains-tax/rates)
- "Crypto gains are taxed at 10% or 20%." Accurate version: 18% or 24% for 2026/27. — [gov.uk: Capital Gains Tax rates](https://www.gov.uk/capital-gains-tax/rates)
- "The reporting framework is a new crypto tax" and "HMRC can't see what's on exchanges". Accurate version: it is data reporting, not a new tax; platforms have collected data since 1 January 2026 and report by 31 May 2027. — [HMRC press release, 7 Jul 2025](https://hm-revenue-customs-hmrc.mynewsdesk.com/pressreleases/crypto-bros-being-forced-to-pay-fair-share-of-tax-3395840); [gov.uk: reporting cryptoasset user and transaction data](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/reporting-cryptoasset-user-and-transaction-data)
- "Airdrops are free money and tax-free." Accurate version: sometimes no Income Tax on receipt, but Capital Gains Tax can still apply on disposal, and airdrops earned for doing something are income. — [HMRC CRYPTO21250](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/cryptoassets-manual/crypto21250)
- "Staking rewards are only taxed when sold." Accurate version: HMRC treats the sterling value at receipt as income, and a later sale is a separate CGT event. — [HMRC CRYPTO21200](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/cryptoassets-manual/crypto21200)

### Inferences
- A worked pooling example would be the highest-value animation here, because same-day, 30-day and pool matching is where beginners go wrong; the three-step order is stable law.
- The HMRC quote uses the word "transfer". The gov.uk list of disposals does not include moving tokens between your own wallets. The site should not teach either "transfers are taxable" or "transfers are never taxable" without the specific manual page. **[ACCOUNTANT]**
- "First reports due 31 May 2027" means HMRC will not hold framework data on 2026 activity until 2027, but platforms are collecting it now; the lesson should say "collecting since January 2026".

### Gaps
- Not verified this session: the rule that disposals must be reported when total proceeds exceed £50,000 even if gains are under the allowance; how losses are claimed and carried forward; the treatment of transfers between own wallets (CRYPTO22100 area). **[ACCOUNTANT]**
- Income Tax rates and bands for 2026/27, and the Scottish position, were not researched.
- Whether crypto ETNs are eligible for ISAs or pensions in 2026/27 was not researched.
- gov.uk and HMRC manual wording above is extracted, not verbatim.
- No dedicated gov.uk page aimed at individuals about giving details to providers was located; the HMRC press release is the source.

## 6. Scams and crime

### Takeaway
UK victims reported £879.8 million of investment fraud losses in 2025 (police figure) and banks recorded £221.5 million of investment scam losses through authorised bank transfers (UK Finance); these measure different things and neither is a crypto-only figure. Globally, analytics firms put 2025 illicit crypto flows at about $154 to $158 billion, roughly 1% of measured volume, with heavy caveats in both directions.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Safe to teach: how to check a firm and what the FCA says**
- How the FCA describes crypto investment scams: fraudsters "advertise on social media, often using images of celebrities to promote the fake investments", run professional-looking sites where prices and returns are falsified, and then either close the account or keep the deception going. Warning signs: unsolicited contact, pressure to invest quickly, unrealistic returns. Extracted; updated 16 February 2026. — [FCA: crypto investment scams](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/crypto-investment-scams)

- The FCA Firm Checker confirms whether a firm "is authorised by us" and has permission for the service; it cannot confirm FSCS or Ombudsman protection and omits some information held on the full Register. Extracted. — [FCA Firm Checker](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/fca-firm-checker)
- The Warning List "shows the firms that we're concerned are working without our permission". "But if a firm isn't on the list, it may still be unauthorised or be a scam." "Unauthorised firms often change their names". Beware clone firms that impersonate real ones. FCA consumer line 0800 111 6768. Extracted; updated 30 June 2026. — [FCA Warning List](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/warning-list-unauthorised-firms)

- Universal rules from the US consumer regulator: "Only scammers demand payment in cryptocurrency." "Only scammers will guarantee profits or big returns." Extracted. — [FTC: cryptocurrency and scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-cryptocurrency-and-scams)

**(a) Safe to teach: step-by-step mechanics of each scam type**

1. **Fake investment platform fronted by a celebrity or deepfake advert**
   1. A paid advert or post appears on social media or in search results using a well-known person's image or an AI-generated video of them. — [FCA: crypto investment scams](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/crypto-investment-scams); [MoneySavingExpert: fake Martin Lewis ads](https://www.moneysavingexpert.com/shopping/fake-martin-lewis-ads/)
   2. The advert promises implausible returns (MoneySavingExpert cites fakes claiming about £200 turns into £35,000 a month) for a crypto or "AI trading" scheme. — [MoneySavingExpert](https://www.moneysavingexpert.com/shopping/fake-martin-lewis-ads/)
   3. The victim clicks through to a professional-looking website and deposits money. — [FCA: crypto investment scams](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/crypto-investment-scams)
   4. The site's dashboard shows rising prices and profits that are fabricated by the scammers' software. — [FCA: crypto investment scams](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/crypto-investment-scams)
   5. The scammers either shut the account straight away or keep the illusion going to extract more and recruit others. — [FCA: crypto investment scams](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/crypto-investment-scams)
   - Accuracy note: the real person never endorsed it. "Martin NEVER endorses products and nor does MSE". — [MoneySavingExpert](https://www.moneysavingexpert.com/shopping/fake-martin-lewis-ads/)
   - Police list "AI-manipulated videos", "deepfake endorsements" and "cloned websites" among 2025 methods. — [Police Professional, 7 Apr 2026](https://policeprofessional.com/news/rising-cost-of-investment-fraud-revealed/)

2. **Romance or relationship investment scam ("pig butchering")**
   1. "A stranger will contact you out of the blue via text message, on social media or on a messaging application", often with an innocent pretext such as a wrong number. — [FINRA, 28 May 2025](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/pig-butchering-scams)
   2. Over days or weeks the scammer builds a friendship or romance with personal messages, fake photos and life details, with no mention of money. — [FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/pig-butchering-scams); [Chainalysis, 13 Feb 2025](https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/2024-pig-butchering-scam-revenue-grows-yoy/)
   3. The conversation is steered to investing; the scammer claims success with crypto and offers to show how. — [FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/pig-butchering-scams)
   4. The victim is directed to "a specific crypto asset trading platform that's likely to be fake and controlled by the scammer", which shows a "falsified dashboard" with fast-growing balances. — [FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/pig-butchering-scams)
   5. "They might even allow you to withdraw early gains to build false confidence". — [FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/pig-butchering-scams)
   6. The victim is urged to "deposit increasingly larger amounts". — [FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/pig-butchering-scams)
   7. When the victim tries to withdraw, they cannot; the platform may demand "additional taxes and fees that you must pay before withdrawing your funds", or simply go offline. — [FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/pig-butchering-scams)
   - Trend: in 2024 revenue rose nearly 40% and the number of deposits rose nearly 210% while the average deposit fell 55%, meaning shorter grooming and more victims. — [Chainalysis, 13 Feb 2025](https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/2024-pig-butchering-scam-revenue-grows-yoy/)

3. **Fake support and impersonation (including screen sharing)**
   1. Contact arrives unexpectedly by phone, email, text, social media direct message or even a posted letter, or the victim finds a fake "support" number or account through a search. — [FCA: screen sharing scams](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/screen-sharing-scams); [Ledger: ongoing phishing campaigns](https://www.ledger.com/phishing-campaigns-status)
   2. The caller poses as an exchange, wallet maker, bank or regulator and creates urgency: the account is compromised, a firmware update is needed, a withdrawal is pending. — [Ledger](https://www.ledger.com/phishing-campaigns-status)
   3. Variant A (screen share): the victim is asked to install genuine remote-access software such as "AnyDesk, Microsoft Teams, TeamViewer or Zoom"; the scammer then sees or controls the screen and gets into banking and other accounts. — [FCA: screen sharing scams](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/screen-sharing-scams)
   4. Variant B (recovery phrase): the victim is sent to a fake app or site showing an error and asked to type their 24-word recovery phrase "to verify" or "restore" the wallet. — [Ledger](https://www.ledger.com/phishing-campaigns-status)
   5. With the phrase, the scammer recreates the wallet on their own device and moves everything out. (Mechanism follows from what a recovery phrase is; Ledger states it will never ask for the 24-word phrase and it must only ever be entered on the device itself.) — [Ledger](https://www.ledger.com/phishing-campaigns-status)
   - Scale: impersonation scams grew "more than 1400%" year on year in 2025 on Chainalysis' data. — [Chainalysis, 13 Jan 2026](https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/crypto-scams-2026/)
   - FCA advice: never share your screen, "even with companies you've contacted yourself". Extracted. — [FCA: screen sharing scams](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/screen-sharing-scams)

4. **Phishing sites and wallet drainers**
   1. The victim reaches a look-alike website (a fake airdrop claim, token launch or wallet page). A wallet drainer is "phishing infrastructure deployed on malicious websites that induces users to sign harmful transactions, enabling rapid asset extraction". — [Scam Sniffer 2025 report](https://drops.scamsniffer.io/scam-sniffer-2025-crypto-phishing-losses-fall-83-to-84-million/)
   2. The site asks the victim to connect their wallet and then to sign something. The request is not a payment; it is a permission (types named in the report: Permit and Permit2, approve and increaseApproval, setApprovalForAll, and newer batch signatures). — [Scam Sniffer 2025 report](https://drops.scamsniffer.io/scam-sniffer-2025-crypto-phishing-losses-fall-83-to-84-million/)
   3. Once signed, the attacker uses that permission to move the victim's tokens out. No password or recovery phrase is ever handed over. — [Scam Sniffer 2025 report](https://drops.scamsniffer.io/scam-sniffer-2025-crypto-phishing-losses-fall-83-to-84-million/)
   - Scale (Ethereum-compatible chains only, lower bound): $83.85 million from 106,106 victims in 2025, down 83% from $494 million in 2024; largest single theft $6.5 million via a Permit signature. — [Scam Sniffer 2025 report](https://drops.scamsniffer.io/scam-sniffer-2025-crypto-phishing-losses-fall-83-to-84-million/)
   - UK exposure: among UK crypto users who met a scam, 40% cited "fake websites or phishing attempts". — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)

5. **Address poisoning**
   1. Blockchains are public, so the scammer studies a target's history "looking for frequently used addresses". — [Chainalysis, 23 Oct 2024](https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/address-poisoning-scam/)
   2. Software generates a new address whose first and last characters match one the victim often pays. — [Chainalysis](https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/address-poisoning-scam/)
   3. The scammer "sends a small, seemingly harmless transaction from this newly generated address", so the look-alike now sits in the victim's transaction history. — [Chainalysis](https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/address-poisoning-scam/)
   4. Later the victim copies an address from their history, picks the look-alike by mistake, and sends funds to the scammer. Nothing is hacked; the victim sends it themselves and it cannot be reversed. — [Chainalysis](https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/address-poisoning-scam/)
   - Worked case: on 3 May 2024 a holder sent about $68 million of wrapped bitcoin to a poisoned address that matched the first six characters of the real one; the funds were negotiated back by 9 May. In that campaign 82,031 fake addresses were seeded and only 0.03% received more than $100. — [Chainalysis](https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/address-poisoning-scam/)

6. **Recovery scams (second-wave fraud)**
   1. People who have already lost money are targeted; the caller may be the original scammer or someone who bought the victim's details. — [FCA: recovery room scams](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/recovery-room-scams)
   2. Contact is unsolicited. The caller claims to be a recovery specialist, law firm, the police, the Government or the FCA. — [FCA: recovery room scams](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/recovery-room-scams)
   3. They say the lost money has been traced and can be returned once an upfront charge is paid, described as "tax, solicitor or administrative fees". — [FCA: recovery room scams](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/recovery-room-scams)
   4. The fee is paid, more fees follow, and nothing is recovered; total losses can exceed the original loss. — [FCA: recovery room scams](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/recovery-room-scams)
   - Tell-tale sign: free webmail addresses. "We never use webmail providers to contact consumers, nor does the Government, law enforcement agencies or law firms." Extracted; updated 19 January 2026. — [FCA: recovery room scams](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/recovery-room-scams)

7. **Fake giveaways**
   1. A post, livestream or message, often impersonating a celebrity or company, promises to give away crypto or to "multiply any cryptocurrency you send them". — [FTC: cryptocurrency and scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-cryptocurrency-and-scams)
   2. The victim sends crypto to the address shown. — [FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-cryptocurrency-and-scams)
   3. Nothing comes back. "Scammers promise free cash or cryptocurrency, but free money promises are always fake." — [FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-cryptocurrency-and-scams)
   - UK exposure: "social media scams including fake endorsements or giveaway scams" were the most common type met by UK users who encountered a scam (41%). — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)

**(b) Needs an "as of" date**
- UK police figures, calendar 2025 (published April 2026): £879.8 million lost to investment fraud; 34,673 reports, up 31%; average loss £25,612; about £2.4 million a day. — [Police Professional, 7 Apr 2026](https://policeprofessional.com/news/rising-cost-of-investment-fraud-revealed/); [Financial Planning Today](https://www.financialplanningtoday.co.uk/news/%C2%A32-4m-lost-every-day-to-investment-fraud-in-2025); primary release (blocks automated access): [Report Fraud](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/uk-victims-lost-2-4-million-a-day-to-investment-fraud-in-2025)
- UK police figures, calendar 2024 (published 8 April 2025): £649,062,146 lost across 25,843 investment fraud reports (reports down 7%, losses up 13%); "Cryptocurrency continued to be the most common asset fraudsters claimed to be investing in, accounting for 66 per cent of all reports"; 36% of reports linked to social media, with WhatsApp in 40% of those, Facebook 18%, Instagram 14%; people aged 35 to 44 most targeted, 55 to 64 lost most. — [City of London Police, 8 Apr 2025](https://www.cityoflondon.police.uk/news/city-of-london/news/2025/april/city-of-london-police-over-649m-lost-to-investment-fraud-in-2024-with-cryptocurrency-fraud-on-the-rise/) (wording seen via search snippet; page blocks automated access)
- UK Finance Annual Fraud Report 2026 (published 15 June 2026, covering 2025): criminals "stole £1.28 billion through payment fraud in 2025, an increase of four per cent". — [UK Finance](https://www.ukfinance.org.uk/policy-and-guidance/reports-and-publications/annual-fraud-report-2026)
- From the same report via trade press: authorised push payment losses £576.4 million across 248,070 cases; investment scams £221.5 million (up 40%), 14,893 cases (up 26%), 38% of all authorised push payment losses; romance scams £39.2 million (up 23%). — [Payment Expert, 15 Jun 2026](https://paymentexpert.com/2026/06/15/uk-finance-fraud-report-2026/) (secondary; medium confidence on the detailed splits)
- UK user exposure (Aug to Sep 2025): 25% of crypto users have encountered or suspected a crypto scam; 7% say they were personally a victim; 87% of those victims lost money (small base of 90). Among users who met a scam: social media fake endorsements or giveaways 41%, fake websites or phishing 40%, schemes promising high returns 34%. Verbatim. — [FCA Wave 6 report PDF](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research-notes/cryptoasset-consumer-research-2025-wave-6.pdf)
- Global illicit volume, Chainalysis (8 January 2026): "at least $154 billion" received by illicit addresses in 2025, up 162%, driven by a 694% rise in flows to sanctioned entities; "below 1%" of attributed transaction volume; stablecoins were 84% of illicit volume. Extracted. — [Chainalysis 2026 Crypto Crime Report introduction](https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/2026-crypto-crime-report-introduction/)
- Chainalysis caveats: the figure is "a lower-bound estimate based on illicit addresses we've identified to date"; the 2024 estimate was revised up from $40.9 billion to $57.2 billion; totals "exclude revenue from non-crypto-native crime, such as traditional drug trafficking" where crypto is only the payment or laundering method. Extracted. — [Chainalysis 2026 Crypto Crime Report introduction](https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/2026-crypto-crime-report-introduction/)
- Global illicit volume, TRM Labs: $158 billion in 2025, up nearly 145%; illicit share of volume fell from 1.3% (2024) to 1.2% (2025); by TRM's alternative "liquidity" measure, illicit entities captured 2.7%. — [TRM Labs 2026 Crypto Crime Report key insights](https://www.trmlabs.com/resources/blog/2026-crypto-crime-report-key-insights-trm-identifies-record-usd-158-billion-in-illicit-crypto-flows-in-2025-reversing-a-multi-year-decline)
- Global scam revenue, Chainalysis (13 January 2026): at least $14 billion received on-chain by scams in 2025, projected to exceed $17 billion as more addresses are identified; average scam payment rose from $782 to $2,764; AI-linked operations took about 4.5 times more per operation. Extracted. — [Chainalysis: crypto scams 2026](https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/crypto-scams-2026/)

**(c) Commonly misstated**
- "UK investment scam losses were £221.5 million in 2025." Accurate version: that is UK Finance's figure for investment scams paid by authorised bank transfer and reported by its member banks. The police figure for all reported investment fraud in 2025 is £879.8 million. They are different measures and neither is crypto-only. — [Payment Expert](https://paymentexpert.com/2026/06/15/uk-finance-fraud-report-2026/); [Police Professional](https://policeprofessional.com/news/rising-cost-of-investment-fraud-revealed/)
- "66% of UK investment fraud is crypto." Accurate version: in 2024, crypto was the claimed asset in 66% of investment fraud *reports* to Action Fraud. That is a share of reports, not of money lost, and it is a 2024 figure. — [City of London Police, 8 Apr 2025](https://www.cityoflondon.police.uk/news/city-of-london/news/2025/april/city-of-london-police-over-649m-lost-to-investment-fraud-in-2024-with-cryptocurrency-fraud-on-the-rise/)
- "Crypto is mostly used by criminals" and, on the other side, "less than 1% of crypto is criminal, so it's safe". Accurate version: identified illicit flows are around 1% of measured on-chain volume, but that is a lower bound that gets revised upward, excludes ordinary crime merely paid for in crypto, and the denominator includes large amounts of trading activity. It says nothing about an individual's chance of being scammed. — [Chainalysis](https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/2026-crypto-crime-report-introduction/); [TRM Labs](https://www.trmlabs.com/resources/blog/2026-crypto-crime-report-key-insights-trm-identifies-record-usd-158-billion-in-illicit-crypto-flows-in-2025-reversing-a-multi-year-decline)
- "Report it to Action Fraud." Accurate version: the national service has been Report Fraud since December 2025. — [FCA: crypto investment scams](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/crypto-investment-scams)
- "A recovery firm can trace and return stolen crypto for a fee." Accurate version: unsolicited offers to recover losses for an upfront fee are themselves a recognised scam. — [FCA: recovery room scams](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/recovery-room-scams)
- "If it's not on the FCA Warning List, it's fine." Accurate version: the FCA says a firm not on the list "may still be unauthorised or be a scam". — [FCA Warning List](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/warning-list-unauthorised-firms)
- "A wallet drainer steals your password or seed phrase." Accurate version: drainers work by getting the victim to sign a permission; no secret is handed over. Fake-support scams are the ones that ask for the recovery phrase. — [Scam Sniffer](https://drops.scamsniffer.io/scam-sniffer-2025-crypto-phishing-losses-fall-83-to-84-million/); [Ledger](https://www.ledger.com/phishing-campaigns-status)

### Inferences
- The scam types overlap in practice (Chainalysis says categories "are becoming less distinct"): a deepfake advert leads to a fake platform, then a "withdrawal tax", then a recovery scam. A sequence animation showing one victim passing through several stages would be accurate.
- Three distinct "what the victim gives away" mechanics are worth separating for beginners: (1) sends money or crypto voluntarily (fake platforms, romance, giveaways, address poisoning); (2) signs a permission (drainers); (3) reveals a secret or grants device access (fake support).
- Chainalysis' $154 billion and TRM's $158 billion are close but use different methods; presenting them as a range ("about $150 to $160 billion identified so far") is fairer than picking one.
- For on-screen addresses in an address-poisoning animation, matching only the first and last few characters and differing in the middle is the accurate depiction; wallets commonly display addresses truncated in the middle, which is why the trick works (the truncation point is general knowledge and not separately sourced here).

## 7. Formalities for the site itself

### Takeaway
A UK limited company's website must show its registered name, company number, registered office address and place of registration; most organisations handling personal data must pay the ICO data protection fee (£52 for a micro organisation); and advertising rules from the ASA still apply to non-technical matters alongside the FCA's promotion regime.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Safe to teach / safe to act on**
- A limited company must show on its business letters, order forms and websites: "the company's registered number", "its registered office address", "where the company is registered (England and Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland)", and the fact it is a limited company (usually by using the full name including "Limited" or "Ltd"). Extracted. — [gov.uk: signs, stationery and promotional material](https://www.gov.uk/running-a-limited-company/signs-stationery-and-promotional-material)
- Legal basis: regulations 24 and 25 of the Company, Limited Liability Partnership and Business (Names and Trading Disclosures) Regulations 2015. The registered name must appear on websites (reg 24); the part of the UK of registration, registered number and registered office address must appear on business letters, order forms and websites (reg 25). If share capital is mentioned it "must be as to paid up share capital". Failure without reasonable excuse is an offence by the company and its officers, fine up to level 3 on the standard scale plus daily default fines (reg 28). Extracted. — [legislation.gov.uk SI 2015/17 Part 6](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/17/part/6)
- Directors' names are optional on letters, but "If you want to include directors' names, you must list all of them". Extracted. — [gov.uk: signs, stationery and promotional material](https://www.gov.uk/running-a-limited-company/signs-stationery-and-promotional-material)
- Data protection fee: "Organisations (including sole traders) that use personal information need to pay a data protection fee, unless they are exempt", under the Data Protection (Charges and Information) Regulations 2018. Extracted. — [ICO: data protection fee](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/data-protection-fee/)
- Advertising rules reach non-paid content: the CAP Code covers "non-paid-for advertising online, such as websites, email marketing and any social media presence you may have, including influencer affiliate advertising". Verbatim. — [CAP enforcement notice on cryptocurrency advertising](https://www.asa.org.uk/static/d04a14c7-b541-48b9-a7f733ef5f9aa8a5/EnforcementNoticeCryptoassetsCryptocurrencies.pdf)
- Split of responsibility after the FCA took over crypto promotions: "while matters relating to misleadingness and other technical requirements in ads for cryptocurrencies and utility tokens will fall to the FCA, the ASA will retain responsibility for issues relating to non-technical elements, such as social responsibility". Verbatim, footnote 1. — [CAP enforcement notice](https://www.asa.org.uk/static/d04a14c7-b541-48b9-a7f733ef5f9aa8a5/EnforcementNoticeCryptoassetsCryptocurrencies.pdf)
- Paid or incentivised content must be labelled: the ASA expects creators to "label their content as an advertisement upfront (including affiliate links) if they get any form of payment", and content must be "obviously identifiable as advertising" where there is payment or a commercial connection. Verbatim, paras 1.9 and 4.7. — [FCA FG24/1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg24-1.pdf)

**(b) Needs an "as of" date**
- ICO fee amounts as shown on the ICO site at 4 October 2026: tier 1 (micro: turnover up to £632,000 or no more than 10 staff) £52; tier 2 (turnover up to £36 million or no more than 250 staff) £78; tier 3 (everyone else) £3,763; £5 off for direct debit. Extracted. — [ICO: data protection fee guide](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/data-protection-fee/data-protection-fee)
- The 2022 CAP enforcement notice required crypto ads to "expressly and prominently state": "Cryptocurrencies are unregulated in the UK", "Cryptocurrency profits may be subject to Capital Gains Tax", and "The value of investments is variable and can go down as well as up". This pre-dates the FCA regime of 8 October 2023 and is historical context, not the current prescribed wording. Verbatim. — [CAP enforcement notice](https://www.asa.org.uk/static/d04a14c7-b541-48b9-a7f733ef5f9aa8a5/EnforcementNoticeCryptoassetsCryptocurrencies.pdf)

**(c) Commonly misstated**
- "A footer saying 'EndorseCrypto Ltd' is enough." Accurate version: the registered number, registered office address and place of registration are also required on the website. — [legislation.gov.uk SI 2015/17 Part 6](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/17/part/6)
- "Crypto ads must say 'cryptocurrencies are unregulated in the UK'." Accurate version: that was the ASA's 2022 requirement; since October 2023 the FCA's prescribed risk warning governs qualifying cryptoasset promotions, with the ASA keeping non-technical issues. — [CAP enforcement notice](https://www.asa.org.uk/static/d04a14c7-b541-48b9-a7f733ef5f9aa8a5/EnforcementNoticeCryptoassetsCryptocurrencies.pdf); [FCA Handbook COBS 4.12A](https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/COBS/4/12A.html)

### Inferences
- A newly formed company with a mailing list, contact form or analytics that identifies individuals is very likely a controller that owes the tier 1 fee (£52, or £47 by direct debit) unless an exemption applies; the ICO's self-assessment tool settles it. **[LAWYER]** only if an exemption is being relied on.
- Stating plainly on the site that the company is not authorised or registered by the FCA, does not give advice and does not earn commission from any crypto firm would be consistent with everything above, but a statement does not by itself take content outside the promotion rules (see section 3).
- If the site ever takes sponsorship, affiliate income or free products from a crypto firm, both the ASA labelling rule and the FCA business test are engaged at once.
