## 1. Centralised exchanges: order book, matching engine, custody, largest venues, proof of reserves, failures

### Takeaway
A centralised exchange (CEX) is a company that holds customers' coins and cash and matches buy and sell orders inside its own computer system, in price-then-time order; the customer holds a claim on the company, not the keys. Binance is the largest spot venue on every dataset checked (26.5% of all CEX spot volume in August 2026 on CoinDesk Data; 38.7% of the top-10 set in Q2 2026 on CoinGecko), and the record of failures (Mt. Gox, QuadrigaCX, Celsius, FTX, Bybit, and Bitget in September 2026) shows that the main user risk is the custodian itself: theft, fraud or insolvency.

### Cited Findings

#### Mechanism, step by step (for the animator)

1. **Orders rest in a book, sorted by price then time.** "Orders are executed in price-time priority as received by the matching engine." [read] — [Coinbase Exchange docs, Matching engine](https://docs.cdp.coinbase.com/exchange/concepts/matching-engine)
2. **A trade happens at the price of the order already on the book, not the incoming order's price.** "Orders are matched against existing order book orders at the price of the order on the book, not at the price of the taker order." [read] — [Coinbase Exchange docs](https://docs.cdp.coinbase.com/exchange/concepts/matching-engine)
3. **Order life cycle has three states: received, open, done.** Valid orders are "received" and confirmed immediately; any part not filled immediately becomes "open" on the book; orders that are fully executed or cancelled are "done" and no longer eligible for matching. [read] — [Coinbase Exchange docs](https://docs.cdp.coinbase.com/exchange/concepts/matching-engine)
4. **Maker and taker.** The order resting on the book is the maker order; the incoming order that matches against it is the taker order (the docs use "taker order" in exactly this sense). [read] — [Coinbase Exchange docs](https://docs.cdp.coinbase.com/exchange/concepts/matching-engine)
5. **An exchange stops a user trading with themselves.** "Self-trading is not allowed on Coinbase Exchange. When two orders from the same user cross, they do not fill one another." (This is a rule of one regulated venue, not a law of all venues; see wash trading in section 6.) [read] — [Coinbase Exchange docs](https://docs.cdp.coinbase.com/exchange/concepts/matching-engine)
6. **Custody: the exchange's wallets hold the coins.** Exchanges describe their own holdings as "hot" (online) and "cold" (offline) wallets; in the September 2026 Bitget incident the exchange said unauthorised transfers came from hot wallets and "its cold wallets were unaffected". [read, partial page] — [The Defiant](https://thedefiant.io/news/cefi/bitget-disables-withdrawals-165-million-one-address-four-chains)
7. **Contrast with a DEX:** Uniswap's own papers describe the protocol as "noncustodial" / "non-custodial", meaning the trader's coins stay in the trader's wallet until the swap itself. [read] — [Uniswap v3 whitepaper](https://app.uniswap.org/whitepaper-v3.pdf); [Uniswap v4 whitepaper](https://app.uniswap.org/whitepaper-v4.pdf)

Illustrative order-book example (my arithmetic, made-up numbers, imaginary token): sell orders resting at 100.10 (5 tokens) and 100.20 (10 tokens); buy orders resting at 100.00 (4 tokens) and 99.90 (8 tokens). The spread is 0.10. A market buy for 10 tokens fills 5 at 100.10 (the earliest order at the best price) and then 5 at 100.20, an average of 100.15. The buyer paid more than the best quoted price because the order was larger than the quantity available at that price. This follows directly from rules 1 and 2 above.

### Inferences
- When a customer "buys bitcoin" on a CEX, what changes is a balance in the exchange's database; the trade is matched by the exchange's engine and no blockchain transaction is needed until the customer withdraws. This is the standard description of how CEXs work and is consistent with the matching-engine documentation, but I did not capture a primary source that states it in one sentence; find one before teaching it as a sourced claim.
- "The exchange holds the keys" is best taught through consequences that are sourced: the Celsius ruling (ownership passed to the company under its terms), FTX (customer deposits were misappropriated), Bybit and Bitget (coins were taken from exchange-controlled wallets). The legal position of a customer depends on the platform's terms and the jurisdiction, so avoid a blanket statement such as "you never own coins on an exchange".
- Recent large thefts (Bybit 2025, Bitget 2026) were reported as absorbed by the exchange rather than passed to customers; this is the exchange's choice and capacity, not a legal guarantee. Bitget's statement is its own claim and was ten days old at time of writing.

## 2. Decentralised exchanges: the automated market maker, liquidity providers, impermanent loss, price impact and slippage, MEV and sandwich attacks

### Takeaway
On an automated market maker (AMM) there is no order book: traders swap against a pool of two tokens, and the price is set by a formula (in the classic design, the two reserves multiplied together must not fall), so every trade moves the price and larger trades relative to the pool get worse prices. Liquidity providers earn the swap fee but can end up worse off than simply holding (impermanent loss), and because pending transactions can be reordered, bots can "sandwich" a swap; measured sandwich losses on Ethereum were about $60 million in the year to October 2025 and falling, while evidence for other chains is thin.

### Cited Findings

#### Mechanism, step by step (for the animator)

1. **A pool holds reserves of two tokens.** Each Uniswap pair "stores pooled reserves of two assets, and provides liquidity for those two assets, maintaining the invariant that the product of the reserves cannot decrease." [read] — [Uniswap v2 whitepaper, March 2020](https://app.uniswap.org/whitepaper.pdf)
2. **The formula.** Earlier Uniswap versions distribute liquidity "along the x · y = k reserves curve, where x and y are the respective reserves of two assets X and Y, and k is a constant." [read] — [Uniswap v3 whitepaper, March 2021](https://app.uniswap.org/whitepaper-v3.pdf)
3. **The price is the ratio of the reserves.** "The marginal price offered by Uniswap (not including fees) at time t can be computed by dividing the reserves of asset a by the reserves of asset b." [read] — [Uniswap v2 whitepaper](https://app.uniswap.org/whitepaper.pdf)
4. **The fee is taken from what the trader pays in.** "Traders pay a 30-basis-point fee on trades, which goes to liquidity providers"; the fee "is applied by reducing the amount paid into the contract by 0.3% before enforcing the constant-product invariant." [read] — [Uniswap v2 whitepaper](https://app.uniswap.org/whitepaper.pdf)
5. **Fees stay in the pool, so k grows slightly with each trade**; the invariant the contract enforces is that the fee-adjusted product must not decrease: (x1 − 0.003·x_in)·(y1 − 0.003·y_in) ≥ x0·y0. [read] — [Uniswap v2 whitepaper, equation 10](https://app.uniswap.org/whitepaper.pdf)
6. **Arbitrage keeps the pool price near prices elsewhere.** "Since arbitrageurs will trade with Uniswap if this price is incorrect (by a sufficient amount to make up for the fee), the price offered by Uniswap tends to track the relative market price of the assets." [read] — [Uniswap v2 whitepaper](https://app.uniswap.org/whitepaper.pdf)
7. **Who provides the liquidity and what they earn.** In v2, "0.3% of all trade volume is distributed proportionally to all liquidity providers." [read] — [Uniswap v2 docs, Understanding returns](https://developers.uniswap.org/docs/protocols/v2/concepts/understanding-returns)
8. **Slippage tolerance.** The interface sets a floor so that "the swap will execute at a rate no less than x% worse than the observed intra-block rate, where x is a user-specified slippage tolerance (0.5% by default)"; a contract that "simply looks up the current price and executes the trade ... is vulnerable to front-running and will likely suffer an economic loss." [read; this documents the v2-era interface, current defaults may differ] — [Uniswap v2 docs, Pricing](https://developers.uniswap.org/docs/protocols/v2/concepts/pricing)

#### Worked example: one constant-product swap with a 0.30% fee

My arithmetic, applying the Uniswap v2 whitepaper's rule (fee deducted from the input, then the constant product enforced). Numbers are made up and are not market prices. — rule source: [Uniswap v2 whitepaper](https://app.uniswap.org/whitepaper.pdf)

- **Before:** the pool holds 10 ETH and 20,000 USDC. k = 10 × 20,000 = 200,000. Pool price = 20,000 ÷ 10 = 2,000 USDC per ETH.
- **Trade:** a trader sends in 1,000 USDC to buy ETH.
- **Fee:** 0.30% of 1,000 = 3 USDC. Only 997 USDC counts for pricing.
- **New ETH reserve:** 200,000 ÷ (20,000 + 997) = 200,000 ÷ 20,997 = 9.525170 ETH.
- **Trader receives:** 10 − 9.525170 = **0.474830 ETH**.
- **Effective price paid:** 1,000 ÷ 0.474830 = **2,106.02 USDC per ETH**, which is 5.03% worse than the 2,000 starting price. About 4.76 percentage points of that is price impact (the trade is 5% of the pool's USDC reserve) and about 0.3 is the fee. At the starting price with no fee and no price impact, 1,000 USDC would have bought 0.5 ETH.
- **After:** the pool holds 21,000 USDC (the 3 USDC fee stays in the pool) and 9.525170 ETH. The product is now 200,028.58, slightly above 200,000, because of the fee. The new pool price is 21,000 ÷ 9.525170 = 2,204.69 USDC per ETH.
- **What happens next:** if the price elsewhere is still 2,000, an arbitrageur can profit by selling ETH into the pool until its price returns to roughly 2,000 (step 6 above).
- **Size matters:** the same pool gives a 100 USDC trade 0.049603 ETH, an effective price of 2,016.02, only 0.79% worse than 2,000. Small trades in deep pools lose little to price impact; large trades in shallow pools lose a lot.

Definitions that follow from the example: **price impact** is the price movement caused by the trade itself (4.76% above); **slippage** is the difference between the price expected when the trade was submitted and the price actually received, which also includes other people's trades landing first; **slippage tolerance** is the limit the trader sets, beyond which the swap fails instead of executing (step 8).

#### Sandwich attack on the same pool (illustrative)

Definition: "In the case of a sandwich attack, the exploiter places two transactions, one before the target transaction and one after. The front-running transaction manipulates the price in the attackers' interest. The back-running transaction reaps the profit of the manipulation." [read] — [ESMA TRV Risk Analysis, "Maximal Extractable Value: Implications for crypto markets", 1 July 2025](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-07/ESMA50-481369926-29744_Maximal_Extractable_Value_Implications_for_crypto_markets.pdf)

My arithmetic on the pool above, ignoring network fees:
1. The attacker sees the victim's pending 1,000 USDC swap and gets a 2,000 USDC buy in first, receiving 0.906611 ETH. The pool is now 22,000 USDC and 9.093389 ETH.
2. The victim's 1,000 USDC swap executes at the worse price and receives 0.394230 ETH instead of 0.474830 ETH, which is 17.0% less.
3. The attacker sells the 0.906611 ETH back into the pool and receives 2,164.88 USDC, a profit of 164.88 USDC before network fees.
4. Important accuracy point: step 2 only goes through if the victim's slippage tolerance allowed a 17% worse price. With a 0.5% tolerance the victim's swap would fail and the attack would not pay. ESMA notes meme-coin traders "typically set high price slippage tolerance", which makes them "targets of choice". [read] — [ESMA, July 2025](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-07/ESMA50-481369926-29744_Maximal_Extractable_Value_Implications_for_crypto_markets.pdf)

#### (a) Well-established facts, safe to teach

- **Why reordering is possible at all.** MEV "originates from the decentralized structure of blockchains, where transactions are not processed in a single, unified queue. This is in contrast with traditional financial markets, where central authorities enforce a fixed transaction order ... Blockchain agents, such as block builders, proposers and validators, take advantage of the absence of a single queue to rearrange transactions in a way that maximizes profit for them." [read] — [ESMA, July 2025](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-07/ESMA50-481369926-29744_Maximal_Extractable_Value_Implications_for_crypto_markets.pdf)
- **Not all MEV harms the trader.** ESMA lists arbitrage (which closes price gaps between venues), front-running and sandwich attacks, and liquidations as distinct types; arbitrageurs "contribute to closing the price gap between the two exchanges, while earning a profit." [read] — [ESMA, July 2025](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-07/ESMA50-481369926-29744_Maximal_Extractable_Value_Implications_for_crypto_markets.pdf)
- **Concentrated liquidity (Uniswap v3, 2021).** "Liquidity providers (LPs) are given the ability to concentrate their liquidity by 'bounding' it within an arbitrary price range." A position "only needs to maintain enough reserves to support trading within its range". "When the price exits a position's range, the position's liquidity is no longer active, and no longer earns fees." [read] — [Uniswap v3 whitepaper](https://app.uniswap.org/whitepaper-v3.pdf)
- **Why it was introduced.** In the constant-product formula of v1 and v2, "only a fraction of the assets in the pool are available at a given price"; liquidity is spread "across the entire price range (0, ∞)", which "means that much of the assets held in a pool are never touched." [read] — [Uniswap v3 whitepaper](https://app.uniswap.org/whitepaper-v3.pdf)
- **Fee tiers.** In v3 "the swap fee is no longer locked at 0.30%"; "the initially supported fee tiers are 0.05%, 0.30%, and 1%", and governance can add more. [read] — [Uniswap v3 whitepaper](https://app.uniswap.org/whitepaper-v3.pdf)
- **Uniswap v4 (whitepaper August 2024; live from the end of January 2025).** v4 "offers customizability via arbitrary code hooks", where "anyone can create a new pool with a specified hook, which can run before or after pre-determined pool actions", plus "a singleton implementation, flash accounting, and support for native ETH." [read] — [Uniswap v4 whitepaper](https://app.uniswap.org/whitepaper-v4.pdf). "All pool state and operations are managed by a single contract, PoolManager.sol"; pools can have dynamic fees that "adjust their fees up or down". [read] — [Uniswap v4 docs, Architecture](https://developers.uniswap.org/docs/protocols/v4/concepts/architecture). Launch timing [summary] — [The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/336276/uniswap-version-4-deployments-to-begin-rolling-out-this-week)
- **Impermanent loss, defined by the protocol's own docs.** "The value of a liquidity provider's stake can go down despite income from fees." Formula: impermanent_loss = 2 × sqrt(price_ratio) ÷ (1 + price_ratio) − 1. "A 1.25x price change results in a 0.6% loss relative to HODL"; "a 2x price change results in a 5.7% loss"; "a 5x price change results in a 25.5% loss". "The loss is the same whichever direction the price change occurs in." [read; I re-derived 0.6% and 5.7% from the formula] — [Uniswap v2 docs, Understanding returns](https://developers.uniswap.org/docs/protocols/v2/concepts/understanding-returns)
- **Other DEX designs exist besides pool-based AMMs.** Batch-auction/intent systems: users sign an order ("intent"), the protocol "groups it alongside other intents in a batch auction", and "solvers are independent, bonded participants that compete to construct valid settlement solutions". [read] — [CoW Protocol docs, Solvers](https://docs.cow.fi/cow-protocol/concepts/introduction/solvers). Request-for-quote systems where "market makers compete to provide off-chain liquidity". [read] — [Jupiter developer docs, Routing](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/swap/routing). On-chain order books (for example "Hyperliquid Spot Orderbook" is listed among DEXs by volume). [read, data] — [DefiLlama DEX data](https://defillama.com/dexs)

[summary, secondary sources that disagree on the amount] — [DappRadar](https://dappradar.com/blog/balancer-exploit-november-2025)
- **Anyone can list a token on a DEX, and most new tokens on some launch platforms were found to be scams.** Solidus Labs (8 May 2025) reported that 98.6% of tokens on Pump.fun collapsed into pump-and-dump schemes and that 93% of about 388,000 Raydium liquidity pools showed rug-pull characteristics. [summary] — [Solidus Labs 2025 Rug Pull Report](https://www.soliduslabs.com/reports/solana-rug-pulls-pump-dumps-crypto-compliance)

#### (b) Fast-changing figures (need an "as of" date)

- **Sandwich attacks on Ethereum, November 2024 to October 2025 (EigenPhi data via Cointelegraph Research, published 4 December 2025):** between 60,000 and 90,000 attacks per month; about $60 million in annual losses for traders; monthly extraction fell from nearly $10 million in late 2024 to about $2.5 million by October 2025; average profit per attack "just above $3"; about 38% of attacks hit low-volatility pools and about 12% hit stablecoin swaps; only six attackers made more than $10,000 in total profit. [read] — [Cointelegraph Research / EigenPhi](https://cointelegraph.com/research/exclusive-data-from-eigenphi-reveals-that-sandwich-attacks-on-ethereum-have-waned)
- **Total MEV on Ethereum, older data:** Flashbots estimated 526,207 ETH (about $1.1 billion) of realised extractable value accrued to validators between September 2022 and early June 2024, about 0.12% of the volume traded on Ethereum DEXs in the period; EigenPhi estimated MEV revenues of $963 million and profits of $417 million from December 2022 to January 2025. ESMA cautions that methodology details are limited. Flashbots discontinued its dashboard after 9 June 2024. [read] — [ESMA, July 2025](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-07/ESMA50-481369926-29744_Maximal_Extractable_Value_Implications_for_crypto_markets.pdf)
- **Arbitrage versus sandwich revenue:** on Ethereum, "arbitrage MEV on average yielded higher revenues than sandwich MEV (USD 20.2mn versus USD 16.8mn per month)", with sandwiching on a "downward trend". [read] — [ESMA, July 2025](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-07/ESMA50-481369926-29744_Maximal_Extractable_Value_Implications_for_crypto_markets.pdf)
- **Solana:** one program, over 30 days from 7 December 2024 to 5 January 2025, "executed 1.55 million sandwich transactions for a profit of 65,880 SOL equivalent to USD 13.4mn". [read] — [ESMA, July 2025](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-07/ESMA50-481369926-29744_Maximal_Extractable_Value_Implications_for_crypto_markets.pdf). A conference presentation put Solana sandwich extraction at $370 to $500 million over 16 months to May 2025. [summary, single non-peer-reviewed source, low confidence] — [Solana Compass summary of Accelerate 2025](https://solanacompass.com/learn/accelerate-25/scale-or-die-at-accelerate-2025-the-state-of-solana-mev)
- **MEV spikes in stress:** MEV revenues rose about 8,000% over three days in March 2023 (USDC de-peg) and about 700% over three days in the August 2024 sell-off. [read] — [ESMA, July 2025](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-07/ESMA50-481369926-29744_Maximal_Extractable_Value_Implications_for_crypto_markets.pdf)
- **Liquidity providers' results, older data (2021):** across 17 Uniswap v3 pools holding 43% of its value locked, fees earned were $199.3 million and impermanent loss was $260.1 million, so providers would have been "better off by USD 60.8m had they simply HODLd". [read abstract] — [Loesch, Hindman, Richardson and Welch, arXiv 2111.09192, November 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09192)
- **Block production is concentrated:** 85% to 95% of Ethereum blocks have been built with MEV-Boost since November 2022; two builders produced 88% of Ethereum blocks in October 2024. [read] — [ESMA, July 2025](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-07/ESMA50-481369926-29744_Maximal_Extractable_Value_Implications_for_crypto_markets.pdf)

#### (c) Contested or commonly misstated

- **"x × y = k, and k never changes."** Accurate version: k stays constant only in the no-fee idealisation. In the real contract the product "cannot decrease", and it grows with every swap because fees stay in the pool; it also changes when providers add or remove liquidity. [read] — [Uniswap v2 whitepaper](https://app.uniswap.org/whitepaper.pdf)
- **"DEX swaps cost 0.3%."** Accurate version: 0.30% was the fixed fee in Uniswap v1 and v2. Since v3, pools have different fee tiers (0.05%, 0.30%, 1% at launch) and v4 allows dynamic fees; the total cost of a swap also includes price impact, the network transaction fee, and any MEV loss. [read] — [Uniswap v3 whitepaper](https://app.uniswap.org/whitepaper-v3.pdf); [Uniswap v4 docs](https://developers.uniswap.org/docs/protocols/v4/concepts/architecture)
- **"Impermanent loss is not a real loss."** Accurate version: it is a shortfall against simply holding the two tokens; it shrinks to zero only if the price ratio returns to where it was when the liquidity was added, and it becomes permanent on withdrawal at a different ratio. Fees may or may not outweigh it; in the 2021 sample above they did not. Sources: [Uniswap v2 docs](https://developers.uniswap.org/docs/protocols/v2/concepts/understanding-returns); [arXiv 2111.09192](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09192)
- **"MEV has cost everyday traders over a billion dollars."** This sentence appears, undated, in a protocol's own documentation that sells MEV protection. [read] — [CoW Protocol docs](https://docs.cow.fi/cow-protocol/concepts/benefits/mev-protection). Accurate version: the roughly $1.1 billion Flashbots figure (September 2022 to June 2024) is total value accruing to validators using MEV-Boost, including tips and priority fees, and it includes arbitrage that does not take money from a trader's swap; measured sandwich losses on Ethereum were about $60 million in the year to October 2025. Sources: [ESMA](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-07/ESMA50-481369926-29744_Maximal_Extractable_Value_Implications_for_crypto_markets.pdf); [Cointelegraph Research / EigenPhi](https://cointelegraph.com/research/exclusive-data-from-eigenphi-reveals-that-sandwich-attacks-on-ethereum-have-waned)
- **"Private or 'protected' routing makes you safe from sandwiching."** Accurate version: a study of Ethereum data from November 2024 to February 2025 found 2,932 confirmed sandwich attacks through private channels (November to December 2024), with $409,236 of victim losses, and concluded "private routing does not guarantee protection from MEV extraction". The same study found about 40% of victims moved to private routing within 60 days. [read abstract; preprint, not peer-reviewed] — [Mancino and Rezzoli, arXiv 2512.17602, December 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.17602v1)
- **Sources disagree on the Ethereum sandwich count.** A search summary of the Cointelegraph/EigenPhi piece said "over 95,000 sandwich attacks" in the year; the article as I read it says 60,000 to 90,000 per month. Use the article's own wording, and note that both come from one data vendor. — [Cointelegraph Research / EigenPhi](https://cointelegraph.com/research/exclusive-data-from-eigenphi-reveals-that-sandwich-attacks-on-ethereum-have-waned)
- **"Decentralised means nobody can stop or seize funds."** Accurate version: the Drift loss in April 2026 came through a small group of human signers with admin powers, and $162 million of the Cetus loss was frozen after the fact; many DEXs have administrators or validators who can act. Sources: [TRM Labs](https://www.trmlabs.com/resources/blog/north-korean-hackers-attack-drift-protocol-in-285-million-heist); [Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/cetus-dex-sui-exploit-200m-loss)

### Inferences
- Confidence on MEV scale is moderate for Ethereum (one main data vendor, EigenPhi, with ESMA explicitly flagging weak methodology disclosure) and low for Solana, BNB Chain and layer-2 networks (ESMA: "extremely limited data"). A lesson should say "measured on Ethereum" and give the period.
- The per-attack profit of about $3 and the fall in monthly extraction suggest that for a small swap in a deep pool with a tight slippage tolerance the expected sandwich loss is small, while large swaps, thin pools and high tolerance (typical of meme-coin trading) carry most of the risk. This is my reading of the ESMA and EigenPhi material, not a quoted finding.
- The worked swap and sandwich examples are safe to animate as arithmetic because each step follows the whitepaper's stated rule; they should be labelled as illustrations with made-up numbers.

### Gaps
- No 2025 or 2026 study of liquidity-provider profitability was found; the best quantified source is from 2021 (Uniswap v3's first months). Treat "most liquidity providers lose money" as unproven for today.
- No reliable, recent, cross-chain total for user losses to sandwich attacks exists in the sources I could open.
- Current default slippage settings in today's wallet and exchange interfaces are not sourced (the 0.5% figure is from older Uniswap documentation).
- Interface fees charged by wallets and front ends on top of the pool fee are not sourced.
- Newer Solana designs that appear high in the volume tables (for example HumidiFi, BisonFi, Tessera V) are listed by DefiLlama as DEXs, but I did not source a description of how they work.

## 4. Aggregators and routing

### Takeaway
A swap aggregator does not hold a pool of its own; it searches many DEX pools (and sometimes private market makers) and splits or chains a trade across them to get a better price. On DefiLlama's data, aggregators handled roughly a quarter to three tenths of DEX volume (about 24% over the 30 days to 4 October 2026 and about 29% over the trailing 12 months, after removing one clear outlier), so routing through an aggregator is common but not the majority path.

### Cited Findings

#### Mechanism, step by step (for the animator)
1. The user asks to swap token A for token B in a wallet or app.
2. The aggregator's routing engine works out a path. Jupiter describes its on-chain engine as "Multi-hop, multi-split swaps across Solana DEXes": multi-hop means going through an intermediate token (A to C to B), multi-split means dividing the order across several pools at once. [read] — [Jupiter developer docs, Routing](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/swap/routing)
3. Several engines can compete for the same order: "all routing engines compete for the best price", including an RFQ system where "market makers compete to provide off-chain liquidity" and third-party routers. [read] — [Jupiter developer docs](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/swap/routing)
4. In RFQ fills the market maker can back out: "market makers have last-look execution rights". [read] — [Jupiter developer docs](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/swap/routing)
5. Intent-based aggregators work differently: the user signs an order, orders are grouped "in a batch auction", and "the protocol runs a competition where solvers submit solutions"; the solver that gives the most surplus settles the batch. [read] — [CoW Protocol docs, Solvers](https://docs.cow.fi/cow-protocol/concepts/introduction/solvers)
6. In a batch, trades in the same pair clear at one price ("uniform clearing prices"), and two users' orders can be matched directly against each other ("coincidence of wants") without touching a pool. [read; these are the protocol's own descriptions and claims] — [CoW Protocol docs, MEV protection](https://docs.cow.fi/cow-protocol/concepts/benefits/mev-protection)

#### (a) Well-established facts, safe to teach
- Aggregators route across multiple DEXs and can split one trade across several pools and intermediate tokens. — [Jupiter developer docs](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/swap/routing)
- Some aggregator trades are filled by professional market makers off-chain (RFQ) and never touch a DEX pool. — [Jupiter developer docs](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/swap/routing)

#### (b) Fast-changing figures (need an "as of" date)
- **Aggregator volume, 30 days to 4 October 2026 (DefiLlama):** headline $213.9 billion, but $139.95 billion of that is a single BNB Chain entry ("CfoSwap") whose entire all-time volume was recorded within the latest 7 days and whose volume is taken from the protocol's own contract event. Excluding it, aggregator volume was $74.0 billion, equal to about 24% of the $312.2 billion DEX-dashboard total for the same period. Over the trailing 12 months, aggregator volume excluding that entry was about $1.064 trillion against $3.615 trillion of DEX volume, about 29%. [read, data; exclusion and ratios are my arithmetic] — [DefiLlama aggregators overview API](https://api.llama.fi/overview/aggregators); [DefiLlama DEX overview API](https://api.llama.fi/overview/dexs)
- **Largest aggregators, 30 days to 4 October 2026 (DefiLlama):** Jupiter $15.0 billion (Solana); 0x $9.6 billion; DFlow $9.55 billion (Solana); KyberSwap $7.85 billion; OKX Swap $7.75 billion; LiquidMesh $4.1 billion; CoW Swap $3.6 billion; 1inch $2.9 billion. [read, data] — [DefiLlama aggregators overview API](https://api.llama.fi/overview/aggregators)

#### (c) Contested or commonly misstated
- **"Aggregators always give the best price."** Accurate version: they search for the best route among the sources they are connected to; the documentation makes comparative claims (for example that RFQ "often beats onchain by 5-20bps on major pairs"), which are the provider's own claims, and RFQ quotes can be withdrawn under "last look". Source: [Jupiter developer docs](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/swap/routing)
- **"Aggregator volume is extra volume on top of DEX volume."** Accurate version: most aggregator volume is executed in DEX pools and is already counted in DEX volume; the RFQ part is filled by market makers outside pools. So the aggregator-to-DEX ratio above is an indication of how common routing is, not a precise share. Sources: [Jupiter developer docs](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/swap/routing); [DefiLlama aggregators overview API](https://api.llama.fi/overview/aggregators)
- **Headline aggregator totals can be distorted by one entry.** The CfoSwap figure above made the 30-day aggregator total nearly three times larger than the rest of the sector combined. I cannot say what the activity was; it is a live example of why a single volume number should not be taken at face value. Source: [DefiLlama aggregators overview API](https://api.llama.fi/overview/aggregators)

### Inferences
- "Roughly a quarter of DEX volume goes through aggregators" is a reasonable statement for late 2026 with the DefiLlama date attached; confidence is moderate, because DefiLlama does not track every aggregator or wallet router and the overlap with DEX volume is not exact.
- Many retail users reach DEXs through a wallet's built-in swap function, which is itself an aggregator (DefiLlama lists wallet swap products such as Binance Wallet and MetaMask in its aggregator data), so a beginner may be using an aggregator without knowing it.

### Gaps
- No independent study was found measuring how much price improvement aggregators actually deliver to small trades.
- No source was captured for the fees aggregators and wallet swap features charge users.
- The share of aggregator volume filled by RFQ market makers rather than pools is not sourced.

## 5. Derivatives: perpetual futures, their share of volume, on-chain perpetual venues, retail losses with leverage, and the UK retail ban

### Takeaway
Most crypto trading by volume is derivatives, not spot: 79.3% of centralised-exchange volume in August 2026 (CoinDesk Data), mainly perpetual futures, which are leveraged contracts with no expiry kept near the spot price by a funding payment between longs and shorts and closed out by forced liquidation when margin runs short. In the UK, the FCA's ban on selling crypto derivatives to retail consumers has been in force since 6 January 2021 and remains in force as of 4 October 2026 (only the exchange-traded-note part was lifted, on 8 October 2025).

### Cited Findings

#### Mechanism, step by step (for the animator)
1. **What the contract is.** "Perpetual futures are the most popular cryptocurrency derivatives. Perpetuals offer leveraged exposure to their underlying without rollover or direct ownership." The trader does not own the coin. [read abstract] — [He, Manela, Ross and von Wachter, "Fundamentals of Perpetual Futures", arXiv 2212.06888, first posted December 2022, latest revision shown on arXiv as 17 September 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.06888)
2. **Funding keeps the contract price near the spot price.** "To minimize the gap between perpetual and spot prices, long investors periodically pay shorts a funding rate proportional to this difference." [read abstract] — [arXiv 2212.06888](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.06888)
3. **Direction of payment.** When the funding rate is positive "the long position will pay the short position"; when negative, shorts pay longs. [read] — [Hyperliquid docs, Funding](https://hyperliquid.gitbook.io/hyperliquid-docs/trading/funding)
4. **Who gets the money.** On Hyperliquid, "funding is purely peer-to-peer and no fees are collected on the payments." [read] — [Hyperliquid docs, Funding](https://hyperliquid.gitbook.io/hyperliquid-docs/trading/funding)
5. **How often and how much (one venue's rules).** "The funding rate on Hyperliquid is paid every hour." Formula: "Funding Rate (F) = Average Premium Index (P) + clamp (interest rate - Premium Index (P), -0.0005, 0.0005)", with the interest rate "predetermined at 0.01% every 8 hours" and funding "capped at 4%/hour". [read] — [Hyperliquid docs, Funding](https://hyperliquid.gitbook.io/hyperliquid-docs/trading/funding)
6. **Margin and liquidation.** "A liquidation event occurs when a trader's positions move against them to the point where the account equity falls below the maintenance margin." "The maintenance margin is half of the initial margin at max leverage, which varies from 3-40x." [read] — [Hyperliquid docs, Liquidations](https://hyperliquid.gitbook.io/hyperliquid-docs/trading/liquidations)
7. **What the venue does at liquidation.** Positions "are first attempted to be entirely closed by sending market orders to the book"; if equity falls below two thirds of maintenance margin without that succeeding, "a backstop liquidation happens through the liquidator vault"; if requirements are met after a partial close, "any remaining collateral remains with the trader." [read] — [Hyperliquid docs, Liquidations](https://hyperliquid.gitbook.io/hyperliquid-docs/trading/liquidations)
8. **Auto-deleveraging (ADL).** In extreme moves venues can forcibly close *profitable* positions to stay solvent; in October 2025 ADL "forcibly clos[ed] profitable positions" and could turn "a hedged portfolio into a naked one during periods of stress." [read] — [FTI Consulting, 24 December 2025](https://www.fticonsulting.com/insights/articles/crypto-crash-october-2025-leverage-met-liquidity)

Illustrative arithmetic (mine, made-up numbers, using the Hyperliquid rules quoted above and ignoring fees): a trader posts $1,000 of margin and opens a $10,000 long position (10 times leverage) in an asset whose maximum leverage is 20 times. Initial margin at maximum leverage is 5%, so maintenance margin is 2.5% of position value. A 1% fall in price loses $100, which is 10% of the margin. Liquidation starts when equity falls below maintenance margin: 1,000 − 10,000 × d = 250 × (1 − d), so d is about 7.7%. A price fall of about 7.7% triggers liquidation and the trader loses most of the $1,000, even though the asset has fallen less than 8%. Funding at the 0.01% per 8 hours base rate on $10,000 is $1 every 8 hours, about $3 a day, which is about 11% of the position per year and roughly 110% of the $1,000 margin per year if the rate stayed there.

## 6. Market quality: wash trading and fake volume, market makers, and 24-hour markets

### Takeaway
Reported crypto volume is not all real: the main peer-reviewed study estimated that wash trading averaged over 70% of reported volume on the unregulated exchanges it tested, US prosecutors have charged self-described "market makers" with selling wash trading as a service, and volume data today still contains obvious outliers. Markets never close, but liquidity is not constant around the clock: it thins at weekends and can vanish in a crisis, as on 10 October 2025 when market makers pulled back and order-book depth fell by more than 90%.

### Cited Findings

#### (a) Well-established facts, safe to teach
- **What wash trading is and the best-known estimate.** Cong, Li, Tang and Yang tested 29 exchanges for statistical signs of fake trades; regulated exchanges showed normal patterns, while on unregulated exchanges wash trading "averaged over 70% of the reported volume"; the fabricated volumes "improved exchange ranking" and "temporarily distorted prices". Published in Management Science 69(11), 2023. The sample period is, from my recollection of the paper, the second half of 2019; confirm on the paper before stating it. [summary; well-known paper] — [NBER Working Paper 30783, "Crypto Wash Trading"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30783)
- **Wash trading has been prosecuted as a paid service.** In October 2024 US authorities charged 18 individuals and entities, including firms presenting themselves as market makers (Gotbit, ZM Quant, CLS Global, MyTrade), after the FBI created its own token (NexFundAI) to catch them; over $25 million in crypto was seized. [summary] — [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/285503/feds-charge-gotbit-others-market-manipulation)
- **What market makers do, and what happens when they stop.** Market makers quote buy and sell prices continuously; in the October 2025 crash they "widened their spreads dramatically or stepped away altogether", and bitcoin's "top-of-book depth [shrank] by more than 90% on key venues", with spreads widening "from single-digit basis points to double-digit percentages". [read] — [FTI Consulting, 24 December 2025](https://www.fticonsulting.com/insights/articles/crypto-crash-october-2025-leverage-met-liquidity)
- **Market makers also fill retail swaps on DEX aggregators** through RFQ systems, with "last-look execution rights". [read] — [Jupiter developer docs](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/swap/routing)
- **On DEXs, liquidity comes from pool depositors rather than (only) professional firms**, who earn the swap fee. [read] — [Uniswap v2 docs](https://developers.uniswap.org/docs/protocols/v2/concepts/understanding-returns)
- **Conflicts of interest are a recognised structural issue.** IOSCO's 2023 recommendations address "conflicts of interest arising from vertical integration of activities and functions", meaning one crypto firm acting as exchange, broker, market maker and custodian at once. [summary] — [IOSCO final report PD747](https://www.iosco.org/library/pubdocs/pdf/IOSCOPD747.pdf)
- **Volume alone is a poor measure of a market's quality.** Kaiko's research argues volume should be read alongside market depth (how large an order the book can absorb without moving the price), because wash trading can push reported volume far above real liquidity. [summary, paraphrased, not a verbatim quotation] — [Kaiko Research](https://research.kaiko.com/insights/crypto-exchange-liquidity-lowdown)
- **Crypto trades every hour of every day, including when UK and US markets are closed.** One visible consequence: perpetual funding on Hyperliquid "is paid every hour", around the clock. [read] — [Hyperliquid docs](https://hyperliquid.gitbook.io/hyperliquid-docs/trading/funding). The October 2025 crash began on a Friday and the forced liquidations ran "in roughly a day". [read; the day of the week is from a search summary] — [FTI Consulting](https://www.fticonsulting.com/insights/articles/crypto-crash-october-2025-leverage-met-liquidity)

#### (c) Contested or commonly misstated
- **"70% (or 95%) of crypto volume is fake."** Accurate version: the "over 70%" estimate is an average across the *unregulated* exchanges in one study published in 2023 on older data; the same study found regulated exchanges showed no such patterns. It does not describe all crypto volume, and it does not describe 2026. Source: [NBER w30783](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30783). (A 2019 Bitwise report to the SEC is the usual origin of "95%"; it is not sourced here.)
- **"Wash trading is a tiny problem: 0.035% of DEX volume."** Accurate version: that Chainalysis figure covers only activity matching its specific heuristics on three chains, and Chainalysis itself gave a range ($704 million to $2.57 billion) depending on method. Estimates of wash trading are lower bounds on what a given detection method can see. Source [summary]: [Economy Middle East](https://economymiddleeast.com/news/suspected-wash-trading-blockchains-2-57-billion-2024-report)
- **"Market makers are neutral plumbing."** Accurate version: genuine market makers provide the quotes that make trading possible, but firms using the same label have been charged with manufacturing fake volume for token issuers, and market makers are not obliged to keep quoting in a crash. Sources: [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/285503/feds-charge-gotbit-others-market-manipulation); [FTI Consulting](https://www.fticonsulting.com/insights/articles/crypto-crash-october-2025-leverage-met-liquidity)
- **"24/7 trading means you can always get out at a fair price."** Accurate version: the market is always open, but depth varies by hour and day and can collapse; in October 2025 one token traded about 35% below its value on a single exchange while staying near $1 elsewhere. Source: [FTI Consulting](https://www.fticonsulting.com/insights/articles/crypto-crash-october-2025-leverage-met-liquidity)
- **"A high-volume exchange or token is a safe one."** Accurate version: rankings by reported volume are exactly what fake volume is designed to improve ("fabricated volumes improved exchange ranking"). Source: [NBER w30783](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30783)

### Inferences
- The strongest evidence on wash trading is old (2019 data) and the newer evidence is narrow (three chains, one vendor's heuristics). The safe teaching point is qualitative: reported volume can be inflated, has been proven to be inflated on unregulated venues, and people have been prosecuted for it; any single percentage needs its study, scope and date.
- No circuit breakers or closing auctions were described in any source read; the October 2025 material describes liquidations and auto-deleveraging running continuously through the crash. A statement that crypto has no market-wide trading halts is plausible but not directly sourced here.
- Because CEXs can combine exchange, market-making and custody roles (IOSCO), and because self-trade prevention is a venue's own rule (Coinbase docs), the absence of wash trading on a venue depends on that venue's rules and supervision rather than on the technology.

- No 2025 or 2026 academic or regulator estimate of wash-trading share on centralised exchanges was found.
- No current (2025 or 2026) figure for weekend share of volume or weekend order-book depth was found; the Kaiko figures are from 2024.
- The identity and market share of the largest crypto market-making firms are not sourced.
- I could not verify the status of CME's 24-hour crypto futures trading (the CME page timed out and the search budget was exhausted).
- The Chainalysis and PCAOB primary pages, the DOJ FTX and Mt. Gox releases, the OSC QuadrigaCX report and the NBER paper page were identified by URL but could not be opened in this session; their content above comes from search summaries and should be re-read at the linked pages before any exact figure or quotation is published.
